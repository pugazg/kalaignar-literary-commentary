Along with those carts,
yoked to powerful bulls,
came monkeys
whom the merchants had raised
like their own children.

Using the shells
washed ashore
as the scene for his imagination,
the poet climbs a mountain-peak
of invention and sings.

The shells have sharp mouths
like the edge of a sword.

Into their bellies,
children and the monkeys
place pearls.

They are Korkai pearls;
therefore the poet compares them
to the teeth of maidens.

The women salt-merchants,
with slender waists described as
“nalgoor nusuppu,”
had soft shoulders and backs
partly covered
by hair divided
into five swaying braids.

Their children,
there on the shore,
put pearls into shells,
shake them,
and play rattles
with the monkeys.

By saying this,
Nathathanar gives such speed
to the poet-heart's imagination
that all who taste the literature
are drawn into a dizzy delight.

Let us praise his skill
and rejoice in pleasure!

<div align="center">*</div>

> “With the salt-merchants
> who came in carts
> drawn by powerful bulls,
> came monkeys like their children.
>
> In shells with sword-sharp mouths,
> they placed the dense-water pearls,
> like the teeth of maidens.
>
> The children born
> to women salt-merchants,
> whose slender waists
> are partly hidden
> by fivefold moving braids
> falling over shoulder and back,
> play with them as rattles…”
