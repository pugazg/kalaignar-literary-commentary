—casting spears
at wild animals,

conducting his hunt,
then turning swiftly home.

On the way
he saw a cleft
between the hills,

looked into it,
and stared in wonder.

There stood alone
a tall gooseberry tree.

Among the small leaves
upon one branch

it bore
a single fruit—
only one.

A turbaned ascetic
told him something extraordinary:

the fruit would be sweet,
and whoever ate it
would keep death away.

The moment he heard
that this was true,

without even
an eyelid's hesitation,

he plucked the fruit
and took it.

The warriors of Thagadoor
assumed their king

would eat the fruit
to preserve his own life.

With broad shoulders raised,
they walked behind their leader.

When Neduman Anji
returned to the palace,

he summoned Avvai,
who sang Tamil
like rubies.

“King,
I know why
you have called me.

Is it not to send me
as your envoy
to our rival Thondaiman?”

asked Mother Avvai.

She said
she was ready to go.

But before she could leave
and return victorious
from that embassy,

the king stretched out
the gooseberry fruit:

“First,
eat this
and taste it.”

Avvai's face blossomed
like a water-lily
under moonlight!

She tasted it
and said only
that it was a delicious fruit.

Then the king told Avvai,
poet of the royal court:

“In the forest
an ascetic said

that whoever eats
this rare fruit
will live longer.

What benefit is there
if my life alone
lasts many more days?

If Avvai's life grows,
Tamil will grow.

I shall live
as long as the name
of Thagadoor remains.

May Avvai live
as long as Tamil lives!”

He had known
that if he told her
the truth beforehand,

she would refuse
to eat it.

Without thinking
of preserving himself,

he wished
Mother Tamil to live.

The fruit
that the man of Thagadoor gave
was the gooseberry.

And the fruit of gratitude
Avvai gave
in praise of Adiyaman's love of Tamil—

this poem!
