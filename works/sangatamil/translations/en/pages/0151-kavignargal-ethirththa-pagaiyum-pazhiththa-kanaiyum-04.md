—and wrote a *puram* poem:

“O Pannan, who gives  
with the bounty of the sky—

may you live  
for all the days  
that I myself live!”

He placed the poem  
in Pannan’s hand

and departed  
toward his capital, Uraiyur.

That Valavan,  
whose heroic history declared

that whatever army  
the enemy brought,

they would return defeated,

that beautiful-faced king  
whose grace flowed like cow’s milk—

one day  
he too fell  
into the grip of death.

On hearing the news,  
as though the drum of their ears had torn,

the gathering of fine Tamil poets  
poured out waterfalls of tears.

“I ask those most skilled  
at making burial urns:

if the urn  
is to hold our King Killi,

must you not bend  
the eastern and western skies,

make the whole world  
the potter’s wheel,

and take lofty Mount Meru  
as the lump of clay

from which to shape it?

For burying the physical body,  
your urn may be enough.

But to contain  
Killi’s body of fame,

this whole earth  
is the fitting urn!”

So Aiyur Mudavanar,  
even while composing  
a lament in grief,

saw that the Chola king’s fame  
had no boundary.

“Ungrateful Death!

How many enemies  
he killed in war

and fed to you  
until you grew fat!

Today you have finished him too.

Who is left now  
to feed you?”

So Aaduthurai Maasathanar,  
telling the story

of the king’s lasting battlefield fame,  
wept.

The poet-queen  
Marokkathu Nappasalai,

a noble lady,  
refused to believe

the news  
that the great king had died.

She flew  
to the palace at Kulamutram.

There the lord  
of the Kaveri country

lay like an elephant  
fallen in battle.

Her heart shaking,  
Nappasalai asked:

“With not a wound  
anywhere on his body—

can the sight I see  
really be true?

Tell me!

Does death possess  
the power

to seize the life  
of the king of Uraiyur?”
