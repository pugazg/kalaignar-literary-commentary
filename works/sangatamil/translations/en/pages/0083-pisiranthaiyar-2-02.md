Making the swan-cock the centre of his imagining,  
the poet wrote a beautiful poem  
in sugarcane-sweet Tamil.

Calling, “Swan-cock! Swan-cock!”  
he opened and showed his troubled heart,

yet restrained the storm within him  
that he still had not received the chance

to see King Perungokilli—  
Kopperuncholan—

and instead spoke of the king's great fame.

Pearls do not become a necklace  
without being strung together.

Yet without even seeing one another,  
the Chola and the poet became a garland of friendship:

Tamil was the gem,  
Tamil feeling the thread.

The broad-shouldered Chola's generosity and love  
became the substance of the poem.

> “Swan-cock! Swan-cock!  
> You set out from the tip of virgin Kumari,  
> with your beak that has pecked at ayirai fish,  
> flying high across the sky  
> toward the northern mountains of snow.  
> May your journey succeed!
>
> On the way, my jewel-like bird,  
> lies the capital of the Chola who subdues his enemies.
>
> Uraiyur, called Kozhiyur,  
> is the capital belonging to my Kopperuncholan.
>
> He guards the land nobly,  
> holding that the wealth of the plough must never be made to shrink.
>
> His right hand gives  
> without the left hand knowing.
>
> If you pass that way with your mate,  
> you will see the light of affection in the Chola's eyes.
>
> If anyone tries to stop you on the way,  
> do not turn back with your mate!
>
> Tell the guards of Uraiyur loudly,  
> so that it reaches the ears of the Chola, our lord—
