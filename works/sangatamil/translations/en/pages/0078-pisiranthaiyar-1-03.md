“Some spread their poetic sail  
in whatever direction the wind blows,

hiding harms beneath praise  
and carrying away enough rewards from rulers of the earth.

But I have come to argue with you,  
to set your rule in order.

Do not be angry!” he said.

“O king of poets, who sings Tamil  
that could melt even stone!

Those around me night and day  
have lost their worth,

unable to offer any counsel or correction,  
nodding ‘yes’ to everything.

You are a great one of concentrated ability,  
come to protect the excellence  
of this fine Tamil Pandya land.

What use is it  
that I merely bear the name Arivudai Nambi?

Does not a government require  
creation, acquisition, preservation, and proper distribution?

Forgetting all this,  
I became a heedless king

with no one to rebuke and correct  
the course of my rule.

Tell me: what must I do?

I shall bow to the counsel you place before me  
and wear it as an ornament,” said the king.

“Listen, king!

Even if the field is smaller than one *maa*,  
if its yield is harvested

and the grain is fed to an elephant  
one mouthful at a time,

with proper care  
that food will last for many days.

But if, instead,  
you give the long-trunked elephant the right

to enter the field itself  
and graze as it pleases,

then in a great paddy field  
the grain will be lost

not only to the elephant's mouth,  
but even more beneath its feet—

and the whole field will be ruined.

If this rule applies  
to an elephant taking its feed,

does not the same rule apply  
to a king taking tax?
