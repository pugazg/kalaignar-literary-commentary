—and in the evening, to cast off his weariness,
Kapilar supposed that the male had left the female by herself,
found some medicine with which to rest in intoxication,
and stretched out to sleep
upon the flowers heaped on the mountain.

If the *mandhi* learned that the *kaduvan* had drunk liquor,
she would scold him and forbid him to come near her.
She would give him advice,
repeatedly insist that he not stray from the righteous path,
and quarrel with him over it.
So, like a husband afraid of his wife,
the *kaduvan* had drunk in secret
and lay there without the *mandhi* knowing.

Kapilar thus imagined the reason
the monkey lay intoxicated.
Yet he wondered: who had brewed for the *kaduvan*
that *theral* which makes the dark-clouded sky seem to spin,
the water-filled earth seem to fly,
and comes like a breeze riding a chariot
to take root in the mind and make it reel?
What fruit or tree-bark had been used
to raise such fierce intoxication
and make him forget the world?

Standing until his legs ached,
letting heavy thought run through this inquiry into the monkey,
Kapilar grew a little thirsty.
He scooped water from the waterfall,
drank a handful and wet his tongue—
and then the poet found the answer
to the riddle that had lain unexplained.
He drank only a little of the spring-water,
but that tasty water raised the pitch
in every pulse and nerve,
and dragged him as far as the palace-gate
of the ‘heaven’ called intoxication.
With great effort he freed himself,
composed a rare poem,
and added it to the collection of *akam* poems.
It spoke of the splendid mountain abundance
of a ruler of the Kurinji country—
