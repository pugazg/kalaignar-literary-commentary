Here the situation was this:
the Pandya king had surged away
to the battlefront.

In the heat where valour was forged,
the vain threats
of the northern wind
had no effect upon the king.

But the queen's condition—
alas, it was pitiable!

The poet Nakkeerar sings:
look at the suffering
of the household fowls,
unable to bear
the cold of the northern wind.

The hen and rooster,
which once wandered the open ground,
searched happily for food,
and spent their days in delight,
now no longer knew
night from day.

Shut inside wooden cages,
they shifted from one aching leg
to the other.

In such killing cold,
with love leaping in her heart,
the spotted peahen tossed upon her bed.

Taking up Tamil
that none could conquer,
Nakkeerar composed
*Nedunalvaadai*.

A lofty tower
like a mountain peak;
its banners displaying colours
like rainbows in the sky.

Upon strong pillars
spread with the hue of blue gems,
was silver itself
painted there as white sandal-paste?

The walls stood
with an indescribable strength,
as though made of copper.

With refined Tamil,
Nakkeerar paints
Nedunchezhiyan's palace.

And the Pandya queen,
whose hibiscus-petal heart
was wilting
with thoughts of him,
lay upon her bed.

The poet now shows us
that bed in song—look!

A fierce war-elephant,
with legs like drums,
had lived a full forty years
and died in battle.

Its two sharp tusks
were taken,
worked into beauty,
and made into an ornamented bed.
Upon it—
