—her bamboo-like shoulders shining,
small earrings swinging at her ears.
Crying ‘Buttermilk! Buttermilk!’
she sells it in the street.
With the money she earns,
she buys rice and other food in the market
and lays a feast so that her kin may rejoice.
From the price she receives for butter and ghee,
she does not desire to buy gold and gems;
to make her livelihood grow and flourish,
she buys more buffaloes, cows and their calves.

Dear bards, if you go seeking her house,
she will, without stinting, cook and serve you
green millet rice;
it will look like the little young
inside the belly of a crab,
and you may eat it with the milk
she scoops out and pours.”
Thus he said.

The poet tells us
that she churns thick curd
with a churn shaped like the cap of a mushroom.
When she churns,
the sound raised by the rope
roars like a tiger in the forest.
The millet she serves the bards mixed with milk,
he says, looks like the tiny young
inside a crab's belly.

Would even ten million greetings
be enough for Uruthirankannanar's imagination—and for his Tamil?
