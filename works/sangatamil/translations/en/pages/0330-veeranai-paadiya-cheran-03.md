Arisil Kizhar, Mosukeeranar,
Ilaveyini, Ponmudiyar—
the list of those who sang of him runs long,
yet he himself rose and stood as a literary poet.

He crossed many fortifications hard to penetrate,
destroyed armies of war-elephants,
and displayed such valour that whether spear-force,
bow-force or chariot-force came against him,
defeat before him was certain.
He made the flag of victory fly
from the forehead of Karur's fort,
yet he was not a man accustomed to pridefully claim
that such fame came from his ability alone.

Once, in a military campaign,
after losing many warriors,
he broke through the barrier,
raised his flag at the gateway of the enemy palace,
and returned to Karur on the royal elephant.
On the road an old man opened his eyes wide,
hailed the crowned Chera king returning
after driving the enemy to ruin,
and walked toward those beating thunderous drums.
“Among those who remain after the dead are gone,
is my son there too?” he asked.

The noble king known in literature
both as Perungadungko who sang the Paalai
and as Peruncheral Irumporai who bore the victorious spear
dismounted from the elephant
and took both hands of the old man.
“Dear elder, old sir, you whose body bends like the young crescent—
what do you desire?” he asked.
The old man replied,
“May I know where the son
who came to battle like a swelling sea has gone?”

“Rather than desire a short life upon this earth,
he has entered the *Puram* poem I wrote
to live there a full life,”
said the Chera king.
With both eyes welling like springs,
he delivered the news
and stood trembling as he looked upon the grey-haired elder's face.
