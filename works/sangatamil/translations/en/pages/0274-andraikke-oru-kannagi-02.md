The great poet Paranar
sang with joy
of this greatness praised by the world.

Because Pegan gave sweet gifts
not only to those who had nothing
but even to those who already possessed wealth,
one might call it “folly in giving.”

Yet he did not show folly in warfare:
he knew the worth of his enemies
and fought accordingly.

In an unbroken waterfall of Tamil,
Paranar raised the giver's fame
upon a mountain.

But then—

as though a colourful parrot
defeated a vulture,
a courtesan of Nallur
brought Pegan down.

She made her two eyes into arrows
and struck his heart;
mixed honey into her speech
and made him a slave of her lap;
bathed him in rosewater
and kept him lying beside her.

Kannagi, meanwhile,
was drenched in tears
and became the very form of grief.

Kapilar, elder of Tamil,
described how the good lamp of the palace
lay without value,
its brightness dimmed
as she wept thinking of the king.

He admonished Pegan:
leave the courtesan's embrace,
he said,
and hurry to Kannagi's house.

He drew that counsel
as a Sangam poem.

2.

“Set out, king,
to stop the tears
that fall in unbearable sorrow
from that golden deer.”

3.

Paranar said on her behalf:

> “To withhold your grace is cruel.”

The poet Arisil Kizhar,
though he had come seeking a gift,
set that aside.

He told Pegan:

“Let fragrant smoke rise
through Kannagi's hair,
spread like a peacock's plume.
Let her wear a garland,
rejoice,
and join you again.
Harness the horses
to the chariot this very moment!”
