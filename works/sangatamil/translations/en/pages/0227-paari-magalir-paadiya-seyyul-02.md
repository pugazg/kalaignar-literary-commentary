—but Kapilar,
lord among poets,
hid those women
from their eyes
and resolved
to protect them
under his own responsibility.

There are people
in this world
who stay near a friend
and taste his good fortune
when life goes well,

but, when suffering comes,
run far away
without even thinking
that such flight is treachery,

then dress themselves
as men of virtue.

Pari,
however,
thought of Kapilar—
Tamil's learned son—
as nectar,

and whenever the poet
was near,
felt as though
he sat beneath
a cool spreading shade.

When news came
that Pari had died,
leaving his wealthy daughters
like birds with broken wings,

within moments
Kapilar himself
became wings
for Pari's two daughters.

Thus all three
flew away together.

He entrusted
Pari's two precious pearls
for a few days
into the care
of people capable
of protecting them
with affection.

Those two radiant gems
were peerless caskets of beauty;

like unchiselled statues
taking form,
like fragrant screw-pine;

like lightning
that tears the sky
between monsoon clouds,

so shone
the daughters born to Pari.

Seated upon the upper floor
of the house
where they were staying,

the flawless daughters of Pari
looked into the sky
at the flood of full-moon light
spreading everywhere,

like milk rising
and overflowing
the mouth of a pot.

Their eyes,
which had wept and wept,
seemed emptied
of every drop.

Yet inside their hearts
the spring of Tamil
had not run dry.

It welled up,
melting the heart,
as the song:

“On that month's white moon…”

“On this day last month,
when the moon rose
like freshly churned butter
and wandered the sky,

our father,
Pari,
a tree heavy with ripe fruit,
was beside the two of us,
smiling—”
