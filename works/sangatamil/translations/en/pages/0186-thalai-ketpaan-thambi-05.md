“The elder brother believed,
‘One who has a younger brother
does not fear an army.’

Yet you cut down
the very neck
he trusted!

You promised
a reward in gold

to whoever
cut off his head
and brought it.

Here is the sword
that cut his head.

Give me
the reward!”

said the poet.

The younger brother
saw his elder brother's sword.

He saw
red blood
upon its edge.

“Brother,
have you fallen?”

he screamed aloud.

The poisonous heart,
the frenzied heart,

shattered
like a mountain
blasted apart

by the news
that his elder brother
had given his life.

The younger brother's heart,
now pure and open,
wailed aloud!

“Even if my life
must be given,

I shall give it
for noble Tamil alone,”

Kumanan of the saffron robe
had declared
in the forest.

“Through the crime
you committed,

sinner,
I learned
the meaning
of that golden saying.

If you leap up,
run to him,

and bow
at your elder brother's feet,

it is not merely
your brother's life—

life itself
will return
to Tamil!

Do not hesitate.
Come.

Touch the sword-tip
and see.

It is not blood.

It is only red colour,”
the poet said.

Why would Ilankumanan
stand there any longer?

Like flies
circling those
who sell honey,

the gathering of poets
who weave verse
from sky-high Tamil

circled around
King Kumanan.

Ilankumanan
fell at his feet.

The generous king
embraced him!

The man
who had agreed
to give his head

as a gift
to Tamil Sathanar

gave his younger brother
another gift:

forgiveness.

The younger brother
gave up the royal umbrella,

saying
he no longer wanted it.

And the elder brother
gave that umbrella
to Tamil!

(*Purananuru* — Poems 158, 159, 162  
Poet: Perunchithiranar)
