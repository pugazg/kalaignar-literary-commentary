—stood beautiful rounded finials
on all four sides,
like the milk-swollen breasts
of pregnant women.

Pearl-and-gem garlands hung there.
There were painted mullai
and jasmine flowers.

A rare, finely worked coverlet
depicted a lion hunt.

The luxurious bed held
bolsters,
pillows,
and reclining cushions
made from swan feathers.

But there—
there was no husband there
to embrace her.

And because of that,
the queen's flood of sorrow
burst its bounds.

Nursemaids massaged her feet
and said,
“Mother, sleep.”

“Go away!
Have you no other work?”
the queen cried out,
angrily rebuking them.

In a painting of the heavens nearby,
she saw the moon and Rohini
close together,
never parting.

“Why was I not blessed like that?
Why must I suffer uselessly like this?”

A tear peeped
through her long eyelashes.
She brushed it away
with a finger,
yet could not erase
her grief.

Thus,
in the palace at Madurai,
the queen suffered
like a flowering creeper
holding honey,
waiting for its bee.

And what of
Nedunchezhiyan Pandiyan?
Come—see him
in *Nedunalvaadai*.

Love did not stand
as an obstacle
to the Pandiyan's sense of duty.

Thoughts of Kopperunthevi
did not make him
swerve from his purpose.

The northern wind
reached his war-camp too.

Yet because of it,
he did not forget
the face of war
where swords were raised—
