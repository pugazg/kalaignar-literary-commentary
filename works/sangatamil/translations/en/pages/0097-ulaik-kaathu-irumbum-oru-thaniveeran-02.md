—she hung a question mark  
at the corner of her eye.

“My beloved,  
clear a doubt for me,” she said.

“What doubt do you have, dear?

Do you want to ask me  
how you came by such softness,

like the feather  
of the swan swimming there?

Or do you want to know  
how honey comes to your lips,

when no bees have carried and poured it there,  
though I scoop and drink it?

Was your face made  
by looking at the red lotus?

Or, my treasure,  
was the red lotus itself made  
after seeing your face?

Can that waist, bending  
like a worm upon a fishing hook in the pond,

support the weight  
of those two rounded breasts?

Or do your shining ivory thighs  
hold it upright?

Are these all the doubts  
that have grown in your heart?”  
asked Vazhuthi.

Before him she lowered her head shyly,  
like a bent ray of light.

“I did not ask a question  
so that you could write poetry about me  
and win a reward!

<div align="right">I did not!</div>

What does the word *eyil* mean  
in sweet Tamil?

Tell me,” she said.

“Oh, innocent peahen!  
Do you not know even this?

Around a capital city  
we build a fortification

to stop an enemy army.

That is called an *eyil*,” said the king.

“I know that too. But one thing—

they say there is an *eyil*  
that no one can break.

A king boasts,  
‘Even this man cannot come near it.’

<div align="right">—and when she said</div>

‘even this man,’  
she pointed at Vazhuthi  
and embraced him!
