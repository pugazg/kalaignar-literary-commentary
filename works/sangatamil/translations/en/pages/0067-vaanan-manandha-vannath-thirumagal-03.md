Seeing passion burn like embers,  
and driven by a great desire to quench that thirst,

he fell upon the bed of a courtesan;  
she took his hand and poured out love upon him.

Morning, evening, night and day—  
without any count at all,

the days passed in the play of desire!

His wife sat in a corner and withered;  
she melted like butter in the desert heat.

When he learned of her condition,  
he hurriedly set out in a chariot.

He prepared a bard  
and told him to calm the maiden's anger.

The musician went to her house  
and explained things with every courtesy.

But the lady of the house,  
with an anger that would not die,

spoke without concealment,  
telling every detail openly

and rejecting her wayward husband.

In a Sangam poem by the poet Palkannanar,  
the heroine tells a bard her condition plainly.

Changing that setup,  
I chose to shape another imagining  
without damaging the core theme.

The warrior left the darkness of the courtesan  
and longed to see the lamp of his chaste wife.

If he sent a bard, the message might fail—  
frightened by that thought, he pondered many possibilities.

At last, he disguised himself as a bard  
and went before his own goddess, Thirumagal.

“Who are you? What do you want?”  
the maiden asked with a glance.

“O lamp of the lineage! Little casket of kumkum!  
The husband who married you has sent me!
