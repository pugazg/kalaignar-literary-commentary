—why did you come here
without reading the signs
and stepping aside?”

The friend,
a young woman
who could match
any youth
in playful speech,

was not frightened
in the least.

Looking at him,
she answered:

“There was once
a time

when you could not
see the sugarcane-like heroine

except rarely,
like seeing
the Karttikai crescent.

You would sit,
hand on cheek,

suffering greatly
because you could not
meet her.

We saw you then
and often said,

‘Alas,
poor man!’

But today—

imagine someone
who enters
a beautiful pond

thick with ayirai fish

to pluck
an aambal flower,

then stands
in the middle
of all that water
and cries:

‘Oh!
I cannot bear
this thirst!’

In just that way,

the heroine
is beside you
without leaving
even for an instant,

yet you behave

<div align="right">as though</div>

you know
neither time
nor hour.

Why this urgency?”

she asked.

“What is
the proper hour
for quenching thirst?

Go and mark it
for your heroine.

Let her memorise it
if she wishes,

or write it
on the mud wall!

O woman
who adds flavour
to speech,

know one truth:

the feeling of pleasure
does not arrive
after first announcing itself,”

said the hero.

“Seeing
how you suffer today,

I wonder
how you managed
to swallow

the impulse
of pleasure

that once nearly killed

<div align="right">you</div>

when, during secret love,
you could not meet
the heroine.

Thinking of that,
I grieve!”

said the friend.

Without waiting
for his reply,

that green parrot
flew away!

He closed
and bolted
the door
that had been left open,

and set about
finishing

the full song
that had been left
unfinished.

The heroine
sulked
without agreeing—

<div align="right">but later…</div>
