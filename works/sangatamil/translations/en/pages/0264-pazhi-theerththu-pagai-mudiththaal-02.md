> “…their faces scarred
> by the pressure of iron weapons,
> the fierce-eyed Kosars of Niyamam.”

<div align="center">*</div>

This is a message preserved
in *Akananuru*.

An *akam* poem introduces
two heroic Tamil warriors:
“Anni” and “Minjili.”

A young Tamil woman bore
a name joining those two warriors' names—
Anni Minjili.

Though she had reached the age
when brows and eyes themselves
become weapons of battle
that bring young bulls down,
she was a maiden of noble character.

Many longed to embrace her.
Some Kosars asked her father
for her hand.
He refused.

The beautiful Anni Minjili
had a chastity that breathed fire,
like the warrior Anni
who died upon the battlefield.

She had the firmness
of the warrior Minjili,
who defeated and killed Ay Eyinan
and made Nannan's town his own.

Like a painted moon,
like a long-eyed kayal fish
moving in water,
the parrot-like maiden
seemed fit for good union.

Some Kosars came grinning,
thinking so.

Her father answered:

“What did you imagine
of my daughter?
My child is sugarcane.
I will not agree
to place her
in the hands of a cruel monkey!

Do you not know
that all her thoughts
are upon the man of Azhundur?

Thithiyan,
guardian of that town,
is her guardian too.

Do not come again.
Understand what I have said
and conduct yourselves accordingly.”

History also tells us
that even among Kosars
famed for truthful speech,
there lived some
with hard hearts.

They devised a plan of deceit.

Many fine fields
growing pulse crops
were the property
of the Kosars settled in Niyamam.

And one cow alone
was the beloved family property
of Anni Minjili's household.
