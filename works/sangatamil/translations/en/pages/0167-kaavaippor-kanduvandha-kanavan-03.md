Without worrying
that the town would spread scandal,

without knowing
dawn from dusk,

he would go on
devouring her charms!

Meanwhile,
the parrot of his home

lay yearning
inside a cage of sorrow:

“My lord
has still not returned!”

Caught upon the hook
of courtesans

who mix jaggery
into their speech,

he would lie asleep,
dazed by the feast
he had consumed!

“Even milk
and clear honey
can become cloying,”
he would say.

But the unfurled buds
of the courtesans'
silken bodies—

those, he called
never-cloying sweet mangoes!

Sugarcane, he would say,
can taste bitter;

but a courtesan's cheek
is the sweetest segment
inside the fruit!

While the noble wife at home,
suffering,
sent floods from her eyes,

he would rejoice,
plucking the veena
of a courtesan's slender waist,

calling it
a streak of lightning!

Again and again
the virtuous wife
who came to share his life

would walk to the doorway,
look out,

and ask herself,
“Is he coming?”

At that very time,
he would be elsewhere,
doing penance

for the privilege
of transferring
the betel from the mouth
of a hired “wife”
into his own!

Once anything
crosses its proper limit,

whatever it is,
it turns bitter.

So too,
what he had considered nectar,

what he had taken
to be “the purpose of this birth”—

the courtesans' affection
and love—

he discovered
lasted only
as long as money
remained in his hand.

Then he left
the woodpecker-birds behind

and returned
to his own home
where the mango-cuckoo sings!

The moment she knew
her husband had come,

her wounded heart
turned into a spear.

“Did you wander
onto this road by mistake?”

she mocked.

“What kinship remains
for you to come here now?

Stop!”

She hurled
burning words at him.

The wife,
usually gentle as a cow,

appeared now
like a leaping tiger—
