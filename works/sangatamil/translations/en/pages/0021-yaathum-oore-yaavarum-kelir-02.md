—would sing rare Tamil poems  
and receive in heaps elephants, armies,

thousands of bags of gold, grants,  
and many other gifts!

Even then, there were some who were like certain poets today:  
they would praise those who lived in prosperity,

but if those patrons fell into distress,  
they would at once flap their wings

like birds leaving a dried-up pond  
and look for the next pond!

Kaniyan Poongundran, deeply troubled on seeing them,  
showed not even a mustard-seed's measure of interest

in fashioning ornaments out of Tamil  
and adorning kings and generous patrons with them.

When some people loudly urged him,  
“Approach the king; compose songs and win gifts!”

Kaniyan answered with maturity  
and firmly declined their wish:

“You sing of the land—good! Good!  
You sing of the kings who rule the land—that too is good!

You sing of the proud-gaited warriors who bear hardships  
and carry honourable wounds

to recover a country or seize a new one—  
very good indeed!

Boundless literatures speak of love,  
and of the love of those who enter the battlefield;

<div align="right">of love too.</div>

So I shall make my path a path of its own,

<div align="right">and begin</div>

to write:  
Every town is our town;  
every human being is our kin.”

Even when the learned Kaniyan Poongundran wrote  
a love poem found in *Natrinai*,

he asked: “When bark from a tree is useful as medicine,  
would one uproot the whole tree?”

<div align="right">That</div>

poet thus offered the world a graceful counsel:  
whatever is useful must be protected.

The doctrine of “one world,”  
the philosophy that all humanity is “one clan”—

in this world, from an age before stone arose and earth appeared—
