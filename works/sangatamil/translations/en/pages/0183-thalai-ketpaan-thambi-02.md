He asked.

Kumanan poured out
what he needed!

The poet continued:

“On the road by which I came
lived a man named Veliman.

I went intending
to receive
what he would give me.

Alas, king!

He died a year ago.

His younger brother,
Ilaveliman,
now lives there,

and refuses to give.

He speaks a cruel reply.

Guardian of rich Mudiramalai,
where prosperity pours!

To humble
the arrogance
of that degraded Ilaveliman,

I need one elephant.

Let me tie it
to his guard-tree.

That base man
must be made
to bow his head.

Give it to me!”

Kumanan gave it.

Just as the heroic Veliman
had a younger brother,
Ilaveliman,

King Kumanan too
had a younger brother.

His name
was Ilankumanan.

“If jewels,
elephants,
armies,
palaces—

if all of them
are poured out
again and again

merely so poets
may live,

our country itself
will become
a mendicant's monastery
without even cloth to wear!

Brother,
I cannot permit this
any longer.

Divide my share
and give it to me now!”

he demanded.

“I have seen
trees on Mudiramalai
drop ripe fruit.

I have also seen
your mouth, brother,
drop red unripe fruit!

What jewels
do you want?

What in the palace
do you want?

Elephants?
Armies?

Take them all!

Give me only
the love
that belongs
to a younger brother
for his elder brother,”

said the generous Kumanan,
and broke down in tears.

But the feeling of enmity
that had put down roots
and grown
inside the younger brother's heart

did not subside.

With a corrupted heart,
he ordered:

“Before the red-rayed sun,
which has ploughed the sky,
returns again,

bow out of my country
and go to the forest!”

Kumanan left.

Among deer,
honey-birds,
peacocks,
cuckoos,
and doves,

there was a purer love
than the love
shown by humankind—
