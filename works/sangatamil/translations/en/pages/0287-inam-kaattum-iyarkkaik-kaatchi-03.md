—help arrived
in the form of a heron.

The crab feared
that it would itself become prey
to the bird that had come
like a friend to save the bee's life.

It quickly released the bee's leg,
ran away,
found its burrow in an instant,
leapt inside,
and disappeared.

Thus the crab too
saved its life.

Fortunately,
if the heron had not arrived
at the proper moment,
the bee that mistook the naval fruit
for one of its own kind
would have reached the end of its day.

“Our companion—one of our own—
has escaped the crab's grip
and returned!”

The swarm, it is said,
raised a great sound
and held a festival of joy.

“One must clearly recognise
one's own kind;
otherwise trouble will pierce the heart
and bring pain.”

Even while movingly speaking
of the lover's grief of separation,
poet Ammuvanar blends in
this beautiful natural scene
and its counsel.

I expanded it in imagination
and felt delight on one side—
yet why did my eyes alone
somehow fill with tears?

<div align="center">*</div>

> “On the broad sandy shore
> struck by surging waves,
> a dark naval fruit
> fell from its low branch.
>
> Bees swarmed around it,
> thinking it one of their own kind.
>
> A many-legged crab,
> mistaking the bee for fruit,
> seized it.
>
> The bee struggled,
> its humming rising like conflict,
> until a heron searching for prey
> arrived, and the bee was released.”

<div align="center">*</div>

<div align="right">

(*Natrinai* — Poem 35  
Poet: Ammuvanar)

</div>

## Explanation of meaning:

**Kilai-seththu** = thinking it to be of its own kind.  
**Alavan** = crab.  
**Imizh** = sound.  
**Asaanthu** = weakened / exhausted.  
**Poosal** = conflict / turmoil.  
**Irai-ther** = searching for prey.
