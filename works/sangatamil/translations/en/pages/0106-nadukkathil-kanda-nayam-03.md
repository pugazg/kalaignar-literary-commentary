—and, like boiled milk  
returning to the udder of the dark cow,

the hero who had abandoned her  
might leave his wife

and run back  
to her own bedchamber.

The beautiful courtesan  
calculated it all that way.

Whether her dream came true or not,  
the good poet Paranar,

who composed the *Natrinai* poem,  
does not tell us.

So why should we worry?

Still—

what grace there is  
in this one detail:

the moment she asked,  
“Shall I tell your wife?”,

that great warrior trembled  
like the side of a drum

marked with *marchanai*  
when it is struck and made to roar!

<div align="right">What grace!</div>

<div align="center">*</div>

> * “Whenever I think of it, I laugh, friend!  
> The man of the town whose deep-water aambal blooms  
> with sharp beaks like the great-clawed rain-bird—  
> he seized my fragrant fivefold hair,  
> and in his quarrel tried to strip  
> the bright bangles from my arm.
>
> With an angry face he came at me.  
> I told him, ‘Do not rage;  
> I shall go and tell your wife.’
>
> Then that fine man trembled  
> like the black-smeared face of the great drum  
> beaten by performers from foreign lands  
> in the ancient hall of Malaiyaman—  
> he who draws long herds of cattle  
> with the bow from enemy lands.”

<div align="center">*</div>

<div align="right">

(*Natrinai* — Poem 100  
Poet: Paranar)

</div>

## Explanation of meaning:

**Vallugir maari** = a bird with large claws.  
**Them-kamazh aimpal** = hair fragrant with ghee.  
**Elvalai** = shining bangle.  
**Oyyum** = drawing or pulling.  
**Mundai** = former / earlier.  
**Pulampiri** = from another land.  
**Vayiriyar** = performers.  
**Man aar kan** = the side of the drum marked with the dark paste called *marchanai*.
