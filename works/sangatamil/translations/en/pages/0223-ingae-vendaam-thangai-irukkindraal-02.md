“Have you come, dear?” she asked.

“Were you distressed,
thinking I would not come,
my love?” he replied.

And he gave her kisses—
one, two,
ten,
a hundred!

She leaned
her ball-like breasts
against him
and said:

“Do nothing more here,
my lord.

My younger sister
is watching us!”

“Where is this sister
of yours hiding?

What a wonder!
When was she born?”
asked the hero.

“This young punnai tree—
I raised it myself.

Because this punnai
has grown along with me,
my mother told me
to think of it
as my younger sister.

So do not brush aside
what I say.

Do not embrace me here!

Do not shower
sugar-candy words upon me,
calling me
‘my firm, precious pearl!’

And as for tasting
my cheek like a fruit—
what more shall I say?

Before this younger sister
whom I have raised,
shyness comes
and stops me
from all such things.

So, beneath the rain of light
poured down by the sky,
shall we seek
another tree's shade
for a festival
where honeyed delight may rain?”

Holding the shoulders
of the bull-like man,
that woman
whose speech was veena-music
pulled him away.

“If that is your command,
can I object?”
he said.

Fondly embracing her,
he held her creeper-like waist
and pressed her face
to his chest.

> into the white sand,
> forgot it,
> and left it there.

> With rich ghee
> and sweet milk
> we raised it well.

> ‘It is dearer than you;
> it is your younger sister,’
> mother said
> of the good punnai tree.
>
> Ah!
> I feel shy
> to laugh with you here,
>
> where, like the soft music
> of minstrels at a feast,
> the great right-spiralled conch
> resounds over the shining waters—”
