—nor did he run
in search of the arm
that wore bangles.

Here,
the Pandiyan set out
at midnight.

For what?
To see the chaste wife
suffering upon her soft bed?

In the winter season
that makes even hills tremble,
the cold wind blew from the north.

Even the flame of a torch
leaned southward.

But the king in the war-camp
to the north—
no, no—
he did not turn south
to perform the familiar duty
owed to the longing Pandya queen.

Sword-bearing warriors,
carrying lights,
surrounded him.

With his left hand
he caught the upper cloth
slipping from his left shoulder.

With friendly familiarity,
he placed his right arm
upon the shoulder
of a powerful swordsman.

In the rain-soaked midnight,
he went through every place
where many great warriors
lay wounded.

With compassion
he asked after their welfare,
thanked them,
and embraced them.

The suffering
of damaged legs,
arms,
and bodies,
received while driving away
enemy forces in the day's battle,
seemed to fly away
when the cool gaze
of their leader fell upon them.

The great queen trembled,
searching for her lover;
he, meanwhile,
walked the war-camp
until his legs ached,
fulfilling duty.

Life divided beautifully
into *akam* and *puram*—
if one seeks evidence
that ours is the precious Tamil people
who have lived so
from that age onward,

is not one *Nedunalvaadai*
enough?

(This was written in simple style
on the basis of the literary work
*Nedunalvaadai*, composed by the Sangam poet Nakkeerar
in praise of Nedunchezhiyan,
the victor of the battle of Thalaiyalanganam.)
