—a painting of a *vengai* tiger
on the waterside pavilion.
Because the painting had been made
so lifelike that it resembled a real tiger,
and because the female elephant's own heart
was clouded by the intoxication of pleasure,
she feared that a living tiger itself
had suddenly leapt toward her to drink her life.
She fled in terror,
throwing into confusion the women who were bathing.
They sprang up from the water naked,
tried to run in panic,
then, realizing their state,
were overcome by shame and scattered back into the water.

Wondering what suffering had befallen his beloved,
the tusker trumpeted and raised its trunk.
Like a commander lifting his sword and setting out for war,
it roared there, submitting to no one.
The mahout struggled to restrain it with the goad;
when all that effort proved useless,
he had no other way.
He brought the female elephant close,
so that its rare love might be fulfilled,
and calmed it.
Like a sailor who quickly applies resin
to repair a split part of a vessel,
the mahout quieted the elephants' uproar
and also ended the trembling of the women.
So, in *Paripadal*,
the great poet Karumpillai Puthanar tells us.

And it was “Maruthuvan Nallachuthanar”
who, they say, set this mango-sweet poem
to the *Palaiyazh* mode.
