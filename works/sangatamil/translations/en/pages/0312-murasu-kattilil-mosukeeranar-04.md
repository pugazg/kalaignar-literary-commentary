The warrior had imagined
the king's eyes would pour fire
and anger would flow in his words.
Instead the ruler said,
“Bring me the fan!”
The guard ran and brought the yak-tail fan.
Taking it in his own hands,
the king gently fanned the poet
and served him with kindness—the ruler
who governed the land with abundant fame.

Until the poet's fatigue had left him and he rose,
the king did not stop fanning him.
After a long while the poet awoke
and suddenly sprang from the cot.
“My king, what is this?” he cried in agitation.
“It is the good fortune this humble man has received,”
said the Chera.

“I know the royal command,
but I lay down without knowing this was the drum-cot.
Though the penalty is death,
instead of waking me with a shout and killing me,
what a sight this is—the victorious king himself
fanning me with a cool *chamaram*!”
the poet exclaimed in astonishment.

The Chera said:
“It is out of the self-interest
that, if we serve Tamil,
we shall live as long as Tamil lives.
You have come into my house
as Tamil itself in bodily form;
this is the service I have done for you.”

The poet offered him in return a poem,
like fruit-juice into which honey had been poured.
