If half the heart were cut away and parted,
death is simply the nature of life.
We were like one heart; but he was
the rising ray, and he vanished in the west.
Why has he not risen again,
pouring grace, to drive away darkness
and raise a pavilion of light in my life?

I cannot forget him;
therefore I have resolved to die.
Yet before giving up life there is one fear.
They say there is another birth. Very well—
what use is any birth to me?
I have given myself over to him.
In that next birth too,
will I be granted this same birth as a woman,
so that I may live without forgetting him?
Thinking so, I suffer without strength;
and because I fear even death,
I grow thin.”

Ammuvanar shaped and gave us
this anguish of the heroine
in an enduring *Natrinai* poem.
Let us drink it as Tamil nectar
and rejoice.

*

> “My shoulders waste; the appointed day has passed.
> Gazing toward the long forest way,
> my eyes have lost their brightness.
> Leaving me, my reason is bewildered
> and becomes something other.
> My sickness grows; evening too has come.
> What shall become of me here?
> I do not fear death—yet I fear this:
> if death should bring a different birth,
> would I then forget my beloved?”

*

(*Natrinai*: poem 397  
Poet: Ammuvanar)

## Explanation of Meaning:

*attam* = forest or arid tract. *vaal* = light.  
*thel avuthal* = to perish. *en neethu* = leaving me.  
*pirithu* = other. *marakkuven kol* = would I forget?
