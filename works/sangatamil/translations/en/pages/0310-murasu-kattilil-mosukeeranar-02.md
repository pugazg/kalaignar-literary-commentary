Hearing that the king was sleeping from weariness,
he waited in the palace to see him.
The poet too felt the need to rest,
so he looked around,
wondering whether there was a place where he could lie down.

He saw a cot, filled with white flowers,
on which the war-drum was placed.
Not knowing that it was the cot
for the drum beaten on battlefields running with blood,
the great poet lay down upon it
as though it were a convenient place to sleep.
It was an age when kings, enraged,
would cut off the head of anyone else
who lay upon the cot where the victorious drum was enthroned,
for that was held a grave offence.

Some warriors had removed that battle-drum
to take it away for its ritual washing.
So the gifted poet, unaware of the circumstances,
closed his eyes upon the empty cot and fell asleep.

Exhausted from walking a great distance,
pearls of sweat forming on his body,
he sank into deep sleep,
forgot himself, and snored.
The king, who had lain on his couch embraced by a peacock-like woman,
shook off sleep and rose.
When the great king came to walk
before the palace, he was astonished,
hurried to the drum-cot,
and there saw Mosukeeranar asleep.

“Shall I bring the sword, my king?”
a man asked, bowing before him.
For there was a strict royal command:
whoever lay upon the war-drum's cot
was to be cut down and deprived of life.
The kneeling warrior therefore waited respectfully
for the slightest movement of the king's eyes.
