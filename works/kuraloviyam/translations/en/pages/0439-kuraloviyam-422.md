But in his calculation, if armies were to clash, they must return carrying the enemy's head—not shaken by the sight of defeat.

The young king stood stubbornly on one leg, insisting that he must leave immediately.

The minister spoke gently:

“Young king! I do not doubt your courage or your diamond-hard heart. We must indeed confront the enemy who insulted us. His crown must be removed. I have no difference of opinion on that.

“Even so, come with me for a little while. Let us walk beyond the palace and return. After that we will decide.”

The young ruler and the aged minister went out together. They walked beside a canal flowing with a soft rushing sound. The young king watched the minister's lips, waiting to hear what he intended to say.

Suddenly the minister stopped. The king stopped too.

“There!” the minister said, pointing.

A stork stood on one leg beside the canal.

“What, minister? Are you playing with me now? ‘Child, child, look at the stork’?”

The king laughed.

The minister too opened a toothless smile.

“Without making a sound, observe carefully what that stork does.”

They moved aside and watched.

The stork fixed its sharp eyes upon the canal water, waiting for a fish to swim past. After waiting a long time, it suddenly drove its long beak into the water. A small kendai fish was caught. The stork swallowed it and stood upright in majesty.

“Prince! Do you understand the stork's skill? During the time for waiting—when the moment is not yet ripe—it remains still. But when the proper moment arrives, it strikes without missing and finishes its task. That is what Valluvar says so beautifully.”

The minister explained the Kural:

One must wait patiently like the stork when the time has not yet ripened. When opportunity comes, one must act as unerringly as its strike.

The young king accepted the elder minister's counsel, took his hands and pressed them to his eyes.

> Be like the stork while the season calls for waiting;  
> when the proper moment comes, strike like its beak.
