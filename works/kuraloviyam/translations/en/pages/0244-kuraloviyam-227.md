—and continued. Though this world turns through many occupations, it moves behind the work of the plough. Therefore, however much hardship it brings, agriculture stands above the rest, he consoled himself, pulling out the thorn that had pierced his foot and throwing it away.

> Though the world turns through many pursuits, it follows the plough;  
> therefore, despite toil, farming stands foremost.

As if singing this aloud, his beloved came walking there with their child at her waist and a basket upon her head, displaying the beauty of her gait.

“Vanchi! What's there for my stomach today?” he asked.

“Only gruel,” she replied in a coaxing voice.

“Oh no—gruel again today? Is this always to be the lot of the poor?”

Speaking these words of pain, he wiped away his sweat and sat beneath the shade of a tree. He looked again and again at the pot and at her. “Must I drink gruel?” His eyes filled with sorrow. She too turned her face away, not wanting him to see that her eyes had become pools of tears.

What a wonder!

Suddenly that gruel turns into ambrosia. He takes the pot and drinks it in great gulps. “Ambrosia! Ambrosia! Beautiful one, you drink too!” he says, tipping the pot toward her mouth as well.

How did the gruel become ambrosia?

The precious child they had brought into the world crawls over, puts its tender little hand into the gruel pot and stirs it. The moment that hand touches it, the gruel becomes ambrosia. Valluvar says so:

> Sweeter by far than ambrosia is the gruel  
> stirred by the little hands of one's children.

Does the gruel become ambrosia? Or does the Kural that tells us this itself seem like ambrosia?

Ah! Even to think of it is sweet. This Kural itself is ambrosia—honeyed ambrosia that never cloys.
