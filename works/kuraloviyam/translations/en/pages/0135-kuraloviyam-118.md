As soon as he sang those lines, those in the hall nodded appreciatively, exclaiming, “Ah! Ah!” The musician's enthusiasm grew. Continuing the two lines he had just sung, he finished with:

> “To think oneself permanent  
> and live like a thorn-bush is another cruelty!”

The great hall shook with applause. At last the performance ended with a benediction for the country. Seated in front of the stage, the king kept looking at the musical master without taking his eyes off him. The musician too was delighted, thinking that the ruler had surrendered his heart to the music. Beside the king, delicious foods had been placed on plates that shone like molten gold. While looking toward the concert, the king kept stuffing the tasty food beside him into his mouth. Adhirasam, akkāravadisal, thenkuzhal and millet flour all kept making their way one after another into his stomach. When the concert ended, the musician rose, bowed to the assembly, came before the king and stood bent in respect. The king placed a pearl garland around the musician's neck.

> “O king of kings who rules the realm!  
> You have given me a pearl garland;  
> from your pearl-like mouth I seek  
> just one word of praise.”

The king replied:

> “There are simply no words enough for praise!  
> I ate six adhirasams—I would say  
> even a thousand would never cloy.  
> Thenkuzhal would conquer even  
> the nectar of the celestial world!  
> And the millet flour—what can I say?  
> I would call it tastier than what  
> Valli gave Murugan!”

Asked about the music, he speaks instead about the food he swallowed! The musical master lamented to the Creator: why had He made such a man? All this time the king had sat in the court out of duty, opening his mouth for the many kinds of food, but never opening his ears to hear refined Tamil music. Knowing about such a man, Valluvar loses patience and bursts out in anger.

What does it matter whether people who know only the taste of the mouth and not the taste of the ear live or die? he asks, his anger overflowing.

> What matters if those who know the taste of the mouth,  
> but not the taste of the ear, live or die?
