Many poets composed verses praising the courage and valour the defeated enemy king had shown on the battlefield.

The victor ordered that only songs praising himself should be heard in the country; at the same time, he turned all poets who had sung of the enemy's skill in war into caged birds.

He issued a stern proclamation that no one should speak of the defeated king's martial skill or fame.

By giving bribes he drew some poets to his side and made them sing of his greatness.

Some of the enemy king's beloved commanders were put to death.

Seeing that other countries did not accept his invasion of the enemy land, his piling up of corpses and his planting of a victory flag on the field, he did not fail to heap many accusations upon the king of that enemy country.

The victorious ruler even drove wild animals toward the camps where the defeated king and his soldiers were staying.

The defeated king and his warriors accepted all these hardships with a smile.

Neither defeat in war nor the loss of strength caused by the deaths of many soldiers made that king and his army tremble in fear and fall at the victor's feet.

Because victory and frenzy had joined together, the victor kept firing arrows of suffering at them. Those who had lost possessed the maturity of mind, firmness and fearless hearts needed to endure them.

What was the reason?

The defeated king's army was an army of long-standing greatness. Therefore neither defeat nor loss of strength could make it lose heart.

Valluvar says that such steadiness belongs to no army except one with greatness handed down from ancient times.

Is this not that army's glory even in defeat?

Here is the Kural:

> In a time of collapse, unshaken and unafraid of wounds—  
> in a time of depletion, this is rare except in an army of ancient standing.

Chapter 77 — Excellence of the Army; Kural 762
