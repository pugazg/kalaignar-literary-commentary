## Visual material

Colour illustration above the prose showing a woman standing opposite a reclining or seated man.

Even before we taste a lemon—merely on seeing one before us—our mouths water, and we are overtaken by the sensation of sourness. But merely thinking of liquor, or merely seeing it, does not set a person swaying with intoxication. Only after drinking it do the dizziness, the lift it gives the mind, and the frenzy of pleasure arise. They say that lemon has the power even to set upright a person who has fallen under heavy intoxication. Lemon can defeat liquor; it can break liquor's hold. Unlike liquor, it has the quality of pouring the sensation of sourness onto the tongue the moment it is seen!

Without saying it outright, Valluvar tells us that “love is like the lemon.” A lover separated from a beautiful woman who offers a feast of pleasure can rejoice merely by thinking from afar of her—the curve shown by her waist, the flavour shown by her lips, the coolness shown by her two eyes. When the maiden who offers a feast of longing stands before his eyes, joy surges merely at the sight of her. Intoxicating liquor has no such greatness; the pleasure given by a woman alone has the special powers of delighting one in thought and gladdening one at sight. Thus the red-tongued poet paints it in pictorial Tamil.

Perhaps that is why women too are praised as lemons. Do not both stir sensation the moment they are seen?

The lemon, which awakens sensation at sight, defeats liquor.

And women in love, who bring joy not only when seen in person but even when merely remembered, defeat that lemon.

For that distinction, here is the Kural:

> Rejoicing merely in thought, and delighting merely at sight—  
> these belong to love, not to liquor.

Chapter 129 — Yearning for Union; Kural 1281
