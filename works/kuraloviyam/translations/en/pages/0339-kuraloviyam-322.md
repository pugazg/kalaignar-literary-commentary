—and walk away. Whenever the young man asked, “Sir, why are you beating me for no reason?”, the officer would punish him further by refusing to open the cell door, imprisoning him within the prison itself and tormenting him. At times he even withheld food and water and left him weak with hunger.

Two years of the seven-year sentence passed in this way. Meanwhile, the appeal he had filed was heard; he was declared innocent and released. After two years as a prison bird, the young man set out toward the little town of his birth to see his parents.

On the road he was startled to see that prison officer lying on the road. He went closer and looked. Blood was flowing from several parts of the officer's body. Lifting him into his lap, he cried, “Sir! Sir!”

The officer opened his eyes and groaned, “Water! Water!” The young man went to the pond nearby, scooped up water in his hands and poured it into the officer's mouth.

“A maddened bull came charging at me, gored me and rolled me over like this,” the officer said, weeping. The young man lifted him onto his shoulder and took him to a hospital. He stayed nearby for five or six days and cared for him until he recovered.

One day, as the young man took leave of the officer, the officer held his hands affectionately and asked, “I did you so much harm. Yet in return you have helped me so greatly! How were you able to do it?”

The young man replied with a smile:

“Sir! I have forgotten both the harms you did to me and the good I have now done for you. Valluvar says that to punish those who do evil, one should do them good so that they are ashamed, and then forget both the evil and the answering good. This applies not only to those who have embraced renunciation, but to everyone.”

Overflowing with gratitude, the officer embraced the young man and asked him to forgive him.

> To punish those who have done you harm, do them good  
> so that they are ashamed—and let the matter go.
