For eight years he worked tirelessly in cooperation with his trusted commanders, building a great force. Just when he was ready to seize the enemy fortress, he was suddenly captured. The enemy rejoiced. False charges were laid upon him and a sentence of death was imposed. Hearing the verdict, he smiled; he called it sweet news; he said honey was flowing into his ears.

But his people did not wish to see him unjustly sacrificed. With tears streaming from their eyes, they convened a great assembly. All who sincerely wanted him to survive and return explained why he must be released and how charges had been deliberately fabricated against him before punishment was announced; they warned the enemy country. At that gathering, one of those regarded as a responsible person rose to speak with apparent sympathy for the freedom fighter.

“Do you know who he is?” he asked—and then answered his own question.

“That warrior, ten years ago, wandered as a fugitive; he committed sea robbery; he was extremely rough in character.”

He went on describing him in this way.

“Even if what he says is true, is the man before us now not a different person from that old one? Is he not now one of the disciplined camp commanders? Why search now for something that will diminish him? Instead of showing concern for his release, is he not displaying greater concern for reducing the man's honour?” those in the assembly complained. “This is speech that gives room to the enemy!” they said sorrowfully as they turned away.

What can be done? Perhaps it was with people like these in mind that Valluvar declared long ago, in unmistakable terms:

“One who undertakes to speak without understanding the nature of the assembly not only fails to know the kinds of speech; he also has no good idea that he is capable of expressing.”

> Those who take up speech without knowing the assembly  
> know not the kinds of words—and have no mastery to offer.

Chapter 72 — Knowing the Assembly; Kural 713
