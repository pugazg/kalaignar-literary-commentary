—from hamlet to village, from countryside to city, is fragrant with the Kural. Just as the saying goes, “even the cock of Uraiyur has valour,” a bear of Tamil Nadu in this *Kuraloviyam* advises Nallan, through the Kural on friendship, that “even in what is heard there is a certain assurance.” Astronauts who have gone to the heavens and returned to earth also praise Valluvar’s chapter on the excellence of rain. Does this not reveal Kalaignar’s great Tamil feeling—that wherever people go, the Thirukkural follows them, and ought to follow them? Such a feeling is only natural in the Kalaignar who created Valluvar Kottam. To see all beings, human and non-human, through the eye of the Thirukkural is our Kalaignar’s Tamil unity.

Kalaignar has been the leader of a great political movement, Chief Minister of the Tamil-speaking state, and a man of many political relationships; he therefore knows the conditions of governance well. For that reason Kurals concerning government occupy a large place in *Kuraloviyam*. He could have brought many more events from actual history as illustrations for the Kurals of Porul. Yet, out of mature civility, we may understand that he has drawn only as much of the outline as the Kural’s perspective requires.

Another excellence of *Kuraloviyam* is that Kurals of love have been given a substantial and balanced place. Kurals of Porul and Inbam are set one after another so that the learner encounters meaning and delight in succession. One page is a picture of the outer world; the next, a picture of the inner world. Tamil itself carries the meanings sweetness and delight, and that meaning comes from the *akam* tradition that speaks of loving intimacy. One may say that only one who knows the inner landscape of Tamil truly knows Tamilakam. Is that not why Thiruvalluvar, who came to teach Aram, also sang the Book of Love with both discipline and flavour?

In Kalaignar’s *Kuraloviyam*, the Kurals of love and desire rise into an epic of fidelity. The backgrounds to the Kurals of Aram and Porul have tangible form and are open to view. The background to a Kural of love is subtler even than thought; the slightest deviation can make it unacceptable. I marvelled to see this great Tamil scholar, steeped in Sangam literature, stand within the discipline laid down by the *Tolkappiyam* and paint pictures of love in a golden-hued Tamil style. Instead of reducing them to the passing enjoyment of the five senses, he reveals for each Kural an inner beauty of inexhaustible union in which body, love and delight mingle. For scholars such as us, this is a feast.

Even medieval and modern poets have not yet properly known or understood the deep *akam* capacity of luminous, sweet Tamil, profound as the sea. Those poets have wrongly imagined and wrongly spread the idea that *akam* belongs only to royal households, warriors and the wealthy. Our greatly renowned Kalaignar sees the truth clearly and makes it clear to others. Farming families, huts by the salt marshes, cattle herders, fisherfolk, woodcutters—even those who sweep refuse from the streets—not only become lovers of the Kural; having learned it, they speak the Kurals of love, pristine Tamil, during both lovers’ quarrel and lovers’ union, mixing and intertwining like milk, honey and fruit until they become one. Kalaignar thus brings back to life the truth of Tamil *akam*: “the young doves of an ordinary family” become the hero and heroine within the Kural’s inner world. However much gratitude we express for this artistic service, our feeling is too great to be contained within the three Tamil letters of the word “நன்றி” — gratitude.
