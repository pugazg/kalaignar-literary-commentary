## Visual material

Large upper illustration of servant Sevval pulling a cart with a family riding in it.

He was a servant in a wealthy household. His name was Sevval. From the oldest person in that house to the children, he was everyone's servant. He had to feed his stomach—how else could a man trapped in poverty live?

The rich man's grandmother was ninety. She would call in a commanding voice, “Hey, Sevval!” The grandson was eight years old. He too called, “Hey, Sevval!” What respect can one demand in a place where one labours as a servant? But Sevval, now fifty, never troubled himself about such forms of address.

He would place the grandchildren in a box-cart and pull them to school. Even when chariot festivals or temple festivals were held many miles away, pulling the cart was his work. Every day when the rich man went to inspect the fields, it was Sevval's shoulders that reddened from pulling the vehicle.

If the cart jolted over a pothole, or if he failed to go as fast as those sitting inside wished, Sevval would routinely receive lashes.

The marks stayed red across his shoulders, back and ribs. The pain while being beaten was unbearable, but only by receiving those blows could his stomach be filled. Only by enduring them could he continue his life.
