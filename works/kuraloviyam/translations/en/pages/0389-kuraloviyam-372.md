Then we may welcome the night that has driven away the day, fondle the jasmine blossoms that come by stealth, roll about comparing whether the drink from her lips is sweeter or the grape wine held in the hand of the sweet-speaking woman, and at sunrise cry ‘Om!’ to deceive the world and nourish the body like gold”—was Valluvar unaware of ascetics who performed penance with such thoughts?

Even today there are stories of senior ascetics fleeing monasteries for their lives, afraid that some ascetic might one day strangle them, bury them secretly and escape by saying, “The great ascetic went directly to the feet of the Lord.” Would not such things have happened in Valluvar's day too?

Valluvar never accepted the false life of that kind of ascetic.

A pure life! A disciplined life that does not deceive the world! A life without attachment! A life rich in character! Such an ascetic comes before his eyes—one born solely to serve.

Valluvar realizes that the man's greatness cannot be counted and described.

Does he say that his great qualities are more numerous than the stars? No.

Does he praise him as possessing more great qualities than the grains of sand in the Kaveri? No.

Then how does Valluvar speak of the greatness of that ascetic?

“To measure and describe the greatness of pure renunciates who have abandoned attachment is as difficult as counting all those who have been born and died in the world until now!”

No stars appear in the comparison. No grains of Kaveri sand. Can anyone count how many people have been born and died in the world until this day? In the same way, he says, the greatness of complete renunciates who have renounced everything cannot be counted.

What wealth of imagination, two thousand years ago!

> To measure the greatness of those who have renounced  
> is like counting all who have died in the world.
