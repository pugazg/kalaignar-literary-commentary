“O God! Have you no eyes? They are beating this speechless creature like this!” Sevval would say within himself and shed tears secretly.

Suddenly a revolutionary change took place in the rich household.

Two bullocks were bought to pull the box-cart.

Sevval had only one worry: would he lose his work?

Nothing of that kind happened. He was given the post of driver, yoking the bulls to the cart and steering them. Sevval could hardly contain his happiness.

Sitting in front and driving the bulls that now pulled the cart, he regarded it as a promotion and felt proud.

He kicked the bulls with his feet to make them move. He struck them with his hands. If he wanted still more speed, he twisted their tails. If the bulls became exhausted after running and slowed a little, Sevval would become terribly angry. He would beat them with his whip until his rage was spent. With the goad he stabbed their backs. Blood would well from their bodies, and flies would gather to taste it.

He had once been a slave and had been beaten. Now he had two slaves of his own.

One night, while he slept, the bulls came to him in a dream. They seemed to speak to him. Sevval listened closely—in the dream.

“Sir driver! The scars all over your body are the marks of the blows you received when you pulled the cart, are they not? How much pain must you have felt then? When you beat and stab us now and twist our tails, does it not hurt us in the same way?”

The instant this question from the bulls entered his ears, Sevval awoke. He resolved never again to beat them and went to the cattle shed. He drove away the flies swarming over their bodies and stroked the bulls affectionately.

Valluvar asks with pain:

“If a man knows what causes suffering to his own life, for what reason does he inflict that same suffering upon other living beings?”

> He who knows what is painful to his own life—why does he  
> inflict pain upon other lives?
