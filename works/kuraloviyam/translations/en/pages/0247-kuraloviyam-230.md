—he wrote that lovers' quarrelling itself gives pleasure to love. That quarrelling is also called `pulavi` and `pinakku`. You seem to be living your married life like a machine without leaving any room at all for lovers' quarrel!

> If there are neither deep estrangement nor lovers' sulking,  
> love is like fruit overripe or still unripe.

You have forgotten that Kural! Try following what I have told you today. Tell me tomorrow what happened!”

Resolved to act that night according to Poonguzhali's advice, Maangani returned home.

Poonguzhali waited until the next evening for Maangani to come. She did not. Poonguzhali herself hurried to Maangani's house. Maangani lay there with tears in her eyes. Poonguzhali was eager to know the reason. Maangani told her:

“Poonguzhali! Last night in the bedroom, as always, he pulled me close and covered me in his embrace. I moved away from him, having planned to begin a lovers' quarrel. He pulled me close again. I pushed his hands away—gently, of course!

“I expected him to ask, ‘Why are you angry, my dear?’ Instead he hissed, ‘Chi!’ Shocked, I ran to him and embraced him. He shook me off and left alone for his village. Your ‘advice about quarrelling’ has brought me to this misery. A fine Kural you gave me!”

As Maangani grew angry even with the Kural, Poonguzhali consoled her and explained.

“Maangani! Valluvar has written another Kural too. Even water is pleasant only when joined by shade. In the same way, lovers' sulking gives pleasure only when it is with one who loves.

> Even water is sweet when joined with shade; lovers' sulking  
> is sweet only with one who loves.

According to this Kural, it is clear that your husband is a man without that thing called love.”

Then Maangani took leave of Poonguzhali and, with the conclusion “Somehow I must live,” set out in search of her husband in the village where he was staying.

Chapter 131 — Lovers' Sulking; Kurals 1306, 1309

`துனி` — estrangement; `புலவி` — lovers' sulking.
