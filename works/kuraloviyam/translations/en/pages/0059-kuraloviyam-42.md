Such interpretations, and interpretations expressing ideas even more regressive than these, have been given for this Kural. The world of reason can accept as fitting the explanation given to this Kural by Puratchi Kavignar Bharathidasan.

The phrase “rain that falls when told to fall” can only mean rain that is capable of falling the very moment it is asked to. A woman who regards her husband as her god is like such rain. To whom? She is like rain to her husband. And what kind of rain? Rain that falls the moment it is asked to fall.

The field lies parched; the crop withers searching for water. At such a moment the farmer looks towards the sky. The rain does not fall—the farmer droops—he suffers in a fire of anguish—

> Rain can ruin; and for those it has ruined, rain itself becomes support—  
> And everything that raises them again is rain.

Is rain not all-powerful! Can human beings live without such rain? What beauty there is in comparing women to the rain that sustains the world! That beauty—the exalted place Valluvar gave—was spoiled by those who wrote the commentaries.

If, at the very moment the farmer thinks the field needs rain, rain immediately pours down, how many families bless that rain! The garlands of praise received by rain that falls when asked to fall are beyond counting.

Such, then, is the woman who regards her husband himself as her god. That is the idea Valluvar blends into richly coloured Tamil. Read it once again:

> She worships no god; she rises worshipping her husband—  
> Rain will fall when she says, “Rain.”
