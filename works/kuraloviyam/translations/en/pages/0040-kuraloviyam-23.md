“With lips joined where nourishing Muthamizh gathers, he blows the flute; to surpass him  
There is no one on this earth encircled by the roaring sea—nowhere!”

The companion who played the yaazh and the companion who played the flute praised one another. They celebrated one another. Thinking that nothing in the world could surpass the flute and the yaazh, the two embraced. Then they forgot themselves.

Is the flute sweet? Is the yaazh sweet? They discovered that there was something sweeter than both. Not at that very moment, but after a year or two had passed!

The family had become two flowers and one bud—

A token of love, a casket of beauty, a picture that cannot be erased and redrawn—it moves about in their home.

When its little hand scoops and dips into the gruel, the gruel becomes sweeter. By touching its parents with its body, it gives them bodily delight.

It proclaims, “Here is the babbling speech that surpasses both your flute and your yaazh!”

When unripe words fall from those not-yet-mature lips, husband and wife conclude that the yaazh, the flute—everything comes only after this child’s babbling speech.

This is the delight Valluvar shows in two lines.

> “The flute is sweet, the yaazh is sweet,” say those  
> Who have not heard their children’s babbling speech.

Chapter 7 — The Blessing of Children; Kural 66
