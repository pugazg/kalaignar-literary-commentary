He was the king who ruled the land. Every day masters of Tamil speech had to come and sing his praise.

Like ants crawling over the earth, countless poets filed into his palace. They piled song upon song in his honour. They folded their hands at his feet.

In generosity, they said, he was Kumanan. In shooting arrows, they said, he would defeat even Kandavan. In courage he was a Chera; in producing victories, a Chola; in unfailing devotion to Tamil, the very rebirth of a Pandya king of the Sangam age. Thus they strung garlands of verse for him.

And he, for his part, gave away whatever came readily to hand.

One day the king looked at the crowd of poets standing in rows, eager to compose verses of praise and richly coloured poems, summoned his council of ministers, and asked a strange question:

"Every rare poet and great scholar in the land under my rule comes before me, worships me with flowers fragrant with praise, and gains reward. Yet there is one man—a poet who wraps himself in the mantle of a lion of self-respect. Why does he still refuse to come here? Why does he hesitate to compose sweet and noble poems upon me?"
