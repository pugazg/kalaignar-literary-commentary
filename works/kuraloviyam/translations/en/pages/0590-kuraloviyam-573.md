—a sudden desire arose in him to display this before that gathering. He began to speak. His address went like this:

"Horses—not merely today or yesterday—have created a heroic tradition in our country from the age of history itself!

"The Chola king Karikalan took his cavalry to defeat the kings Kanaka and Vijaya. After defeating them, he took stone from the Vindhya mountains, placed it upon their heads, brought it to Madurai, and erected a statue for Madhavi. Were not horses what made that great victory possible?

"Likewise, when a Pallava king made war upon Lanka, brought back many thousands of prisoners and used them to build the Kallanai across the Vaigai river, that too was done by cavalry!"

Those seated on the festival stage struggled to suppress explosive laughter as they listened to this speech by the Patron of Steeds. Whatever else he might be, he was the hero of the occasion. How could they criticize his speech to his face?

So they waited for the function to end. The moment it did, they dispersed with great sighs of relief.

As they left, they spoke among themselves.

"The man has turned history upside down and mixed everything together!"

"People say he reads a great deal! Only now do we understand the quality of that learning."

"Thiruvalluvar did not say it for nothing!"

"What did he say?"

"Valluvar composed a Kural saying that when ignorant people speak about things they have never learned and present themselves as though they were learned, they make others doubt even whether the things they claim already to know were learned properly."

"He must have written it especially for people like this man."

Talking in this way, the eminent guests who had attended the function left the hall.

> To take up and display what one has never learned  
> makes even one's thoroughly learned knowledge seem doubtful.
