Planning to intercept the enemy on the way and drive them back, the king had his soldiers form ranks at the fort entrance and ordered them to be ready to move the moment the command was given.

Warriors from the capital and from the surrounding villages kept arriving one after another on horseback and joining that battle formation.

Villavan too, eager to reach the formation at the appointed time, kept urging his horse forward. But what he expected did not happen. Quite unexpectedly, the horse he was riding suddenly sprang up, bucked this way and that, threw him to the ground and ran away. More than the pain of the fall, Villavan could not bear the shock of the horse throwing him down and fleeing. Even without the horse he longed to run and join the formation. Faster than the wind, he ran to join the soldiers of his own land and defeat the foreign enemy.

Valluvar stood watching this scene. A horse raised with so much trust and affection! A horse that had become so accustomed to its rider! Yet at the crucial moment it not only failed to help him, but threw him down and ran away.

Alas! How deeply that warrior's heart must have been broken by what his horse had done. Valluvar's mind dwelt on all this. He wondered what this scene might be compared with. From that thought was born the Kural that advises: “Rather than the friendship of those who, like a horse, throw one down on the battlefield and flee when war arrives, it is better to remain alone without such friendship.” This is that Kural:

> Like an untrained horse that casts one off in battle,  
> better than such companions is solitude.

Chapter 82 — Evil Friendship; Kural 814
