If Tolkappiyar, the learned pupil of Athankottu Asan, composed a grammar for Tamil four thousand years ago, must not our language—our classical Tamil language—already have been a language that had grown for at least four thousand years before that?

Is ours not the ancient Tamil people who, as the saying goes, arose before stone appeared and before soil came into being?

Are Tamils not the proud people who shaped literature for life itself under the categories of akam and puram?

Did not Ilango, using words sweet as sugar candy, shape Kannagi's story into the epic Silappathikaram?

And did not his elder brother Senguttuvan march his army as far as the Himalayas, make Kanaka and Vijaya carry a stone upon their heads from Kuyilaluvam, and fashion from it a statue for that queen of chastity?

Are these not glories belonging to the Tamil people?

A Tamil youth stood in the middle of an assembly and spoke loudly, piling up the greatness of his people in this way.

At first hundreds had gathered in the hall.

But as the youth continued speaking, the assembly began to thin.

One by one people left with curled lips, furrowed foreheads and widened eyes. Their mouths muttered as they went:

“Yes, yes! What a great race he has found! The Tamil people, indeed! Tin-pot people! He is babbling like a madman.”

Everyone left, talking and mocking like this.

Only one bearded elder remained.

His long beard was white like sea foam. His body shone golden. He had the appearance of a lion.

The youth approached him with sorrow on his face.

“Elder! I am speaking about our Tamil people. Everyone who was in the assembly has gone away. Why?”

The elder replied:

“Younger brother! Valluvar wrote that speaking before a group that is not of one's own kind is like pouring nectar into a filthy, unclean courtyard. Have you forgotten that?”

He patted the youth and left.

> Speaking before those who are not of one's own company  
> is like nectar spilled into a filthy courtyard.
