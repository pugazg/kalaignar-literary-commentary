She sees it. Pearls of tears roll down. The pearls between her lips seem to hide somewhere. Grieving over the separation and angry with her lover, she lies down on the bed—or rather, falls upon it, dropping her body with a thud. Sleep struggles to touch her, but at last it wins. She sleeps.

Her lover comes. She presses her wondrously beautiful face against his broad chest. He drenches her from crown to sole in a rain of kisses. Time passes unnoticed. Delight without interruption! Play! Song! Suddenly she wakes—and with him the sun too seems to wake. It was all a dream. Disturbed, she comes and stands at the doorway.

Seeing her, the people of the village say as they pass, “Poor thing! Her lover has left her. That is why this maiden stands so distraught.”

She takes their words as blame directed at her lover. In her mind, no one but she has the right to find fault with him. She turns her blame upon the villagers who speak against the lover who has gone away. How?

“These townspeople accuse my beloved of being away from me. Do they never see in dreams the lovers from whom they themselves are separated?” she asks in mocking wonder.

Here is the Kural:

> “He has forsaken her in waking life,” these townspeople say;  
> do they never see their own beloved in dreams?

Chapter 122 — Speaking of the Dream State; Kural 1220
