When she went to the pond to bathe, even women of her own age would look upon her beauty in wonder. The whole town yearned to know whom she loved, to whom she had given herself, and who the fortunate man was who had received such a blessing.

Yet no one tasted that fruit of delight. She was not prepared to give anyone that opportunity. She did not wish to diminish her incomparable beauty. She would admire her own loveliness in the mirror and take pride in it. She believed that if a man's hand touched her, her figure would be spoiled and her beauty would decline. So she gave no man consent and allowed no room even for such talk.

Days passed. Years rolled on. Before a wedding celebration ever came, the age for a sixtieth-year celebration arrived. That statue of beauty was now sixty years old. The beauty she had guarded out of fear that the touch of a young man would destroy it was slowly dissolved instead by the hand of time. She became old—a walking, ripened fruit marked by grey hair, wrinkles and age.

Valluvar tells the story of such a woman movingly in a single line.

“A woman of surpassing beauty lived alone and grew old.” That is the story of a woman told by Valluvar. Do you know why he tells it? It is not merely a story. It is an incomparable simile.

“The wealth of one who does not give even one thing to help the destitute is like a beautiful woman living alone and growing old.”

Here is that Kural:

> The wealth of one who gives nothing to the destitute  
> is like great beauty possessed by a woman who grows old alone.

Chapter 101 — Wealth Without Beneficence; Kural 1007
