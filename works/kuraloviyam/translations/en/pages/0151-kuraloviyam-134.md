Both men were married. Thelindhan's wife and Nelindhan's wife were women of such beauty that each could rival the other.

Even within that intimate friendship, evil thoughts about Thelindhan's wife arose in Nelindhan's heart. Thelindhan did not realize that such a sickness was growing in his friend's mind. His wife too was innocent, without deceit or guile. She did not notice the signs of lust that had settled in Nelindhan's gaze. Nelindhan kept yearning somehow to fulfil his desire one day.

As he had hoped, one day circumstances turned favourable for his attempt. His friend Thelindhan had to travel to another town on palace business. That day Nelindhan went to Thelindhan's house. Thelindhan's wife welcomed him and asked him to sit in the front hall. “Would you like something to drink?” she asked affectionately.

“I need nothing now,” he said, frenzy dancing in his eyes.

“After coming to our house, how can you leave without drinking anything? There is milk; I will bring some.”

“No! No! Do you know the third paal in the Thirukkural?”

“What are you saying?”

“That is the Kamathuppaal—the Book of Love!”

Saying this, he rushed at her and began to embrace her. For a moment she was stunned. Then she became a volcano—the fury of the sea—and, with all the strength in her hands, struck his cheeks again and again. Nelindhan bent and crumpled, true to his name. He tried to run. She looked at him and spoke loudly:

“You ask whether I know the third paal of the Kural? Fool! Read the first paal, the Book of Aram! Then you will understand!

> Among all who stand outside virtue, none are more foolish  
> than those who stand at another man's door desiring his wife.

Do you understand the meaning of this Kural? Listen, I will tell you! Of all those who abandon virtue and stand in evil ways, the greatest fools are those who desire another man's wife and go stand at the doorway of that house. Yes! There are none so ignorant of virtue as they are.”
