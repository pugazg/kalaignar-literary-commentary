As time passed, poverty gnawed away at Porko's family. His parents returned to the earth.

He reached a state in which he could not even buy a change of clothes for the wife who had trusted him and joined his life. He wrung his hands and wept. People whose hearts melted in compassion for Porko helped him as much as they could, but they themselves were not wealthy enough to keep helping forever.

One day they told him:

“Your childhood friend Nallithayan lives with limitless wealth. If you go to him and explain your condition, your life may bloom again.”

Though his wife tried to stop him, Porko would not listen. He set out to seek Nallithayan.

He waited at the entrance of the mansion from morning until evening. At last he received an opportunity to meet his friend.

In the beautiful courtyard of the mansion, Nallithayan reclined upon a golden swing, his head resting in the lap of a woman with kovai-fruit lips, his hand upon her breast. He looked mockingly at Porko, who stood before him.

“Come, Porko! Come! What is this? I expected you to come with prosperity. Instead you have come looking like a man carrying a beggar's bowl!”

He released a mischievous laugh, and with it a bud of arrogance opened. The question burned Porko's heart like a fiery arrow.

Porko looked at his old friend and answered angrily. His answer was a Kural:

> Mockery is painful even in jest; even amid enmity,  
> those who know good conduct preserve what is fitting.

Having recited the Kural, Porko left.

The mango-like beauty in Nallithayan's lap rubbed her cheek against his and asked:

“Beloved! What does the verse he recited before leaving mean?”

“Even when hostility is present, good character lies in understanding another person's nature and acting accordingly. Since speaking contemptuously of someone—even as a joke—causes pain, such speech is not good conduct.
