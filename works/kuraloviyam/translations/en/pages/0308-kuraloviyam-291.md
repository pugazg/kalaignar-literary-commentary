Evening came; the harvest work ended; everyone returned home. Kadamban had to keep watch over the threshing ground that night. He had told Kaali to come there after dark.

Would she fail to come?

She cursed the darkness for taking so long to arrive.

As though frightened of her, darkness rushed in. Like a thieving cat, Kaali slipped stealthily between the heap of harvested paddy and the stack of straw left after threshing. She had come like a cat; there she stood transformed into a flower.

Kadamban became a bee and drank honey. Neither the dust of the threshing ground nor the itch caused by their bodies pressing into straw seemed an obstacle to their revelry of pleasure. They were no longer in this world at all. He in her lap; she upon his shoulder; the sweet sound of kisses.

All night they lay intoxicated by the full taste of the three fruits, as though jackfruit pulp were mixed with honey alongside mango and banana. Had dawn never come, their pleasure would have continued without end.

The next day, on the landlord's orders, Kadamban left for a neighbouring town on urgent work. They said it would take six or seven days for him to return.

Now it is clear why Vanchikkodi lay withered, is it not? Women sometimes feel an unnecessary anger toward the lover who has gone away. It is natural—perhaps an effect of the anguish of separation. Kaali too became angry. Her heart said to her:

“Kaali! Kadamban, who left you behind to suffer—there he comes. When he arrives, tell him firmly that you have no relationship with him!”

Kaali laughed. Anger was mixed into that laugh. She spoke:

“My heart! You, who belong to me, left me and went with him the moment he departed, and only now have returned. If you yourself forgot your bond with me, there is no great fault in his forgetting his bond and leaving.”

Thus Kaali reproached her own heart and, in anger, called even her departed lover an “outsider.”

By then Kadamban came running and embraced her. The word “outsider” was struck down. The fish was caught in the net of his love.

> If one's own heart is not one's friend,  
> what refuge can there be in strangers who are not friends?
