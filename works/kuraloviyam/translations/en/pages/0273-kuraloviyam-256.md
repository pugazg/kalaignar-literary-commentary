When the story ended, the lady of the house and the maid returned home before the Bhagavathar. The Bhagavathar and the wealthy host reached the house a little later.

A comfortable private room upstairs had been prepared for the Bhagavathar. Before he came up after his meal, the host instructed the maid to clean the upstairs room and straighten the bed. She went and began doing that work.

After eating, the Bhagavathar covered his body with sandal paste and entered the upstairs room.

The moment he saw the maid there, he lost himself. He poured out all the descriptions of Sita that he had piled up during the kathakalakshepam. Finally he even tried to touch her.

The maid flared in anger. Looking at the Bhagavathar, she spat out a contemptuous “Thu!” and ran downstairs.

The mistress of the house—the wealthy man's wife—came upstairs carrying milk for the Bhagavathar in a silver bowl. Seeing that his face appeared strangely dejected, she asked, “Why? What is the matter?”

“Fatigue from giving the discourse. My body aches, that is all,” said the Bhagavathar.

“Shall I massage your body?” asked the lady. Would the Bhagavathar refuse? The woman who began to massage him was caught in his embrace. What she desired took place.

What the woman branded as “lower caste” would not accept, the woman branded as “upper caste” willingly accepted. High birth and low birth do not arise from caste systems that human beings have constructed.

To live with good conduct is the sign of noble birth. Whoever departs from good conduct, whatever their caste, is thereby of degraded birth. By the Kural's measure, the maid was of noble birth; the wealthy mistress of the house was of degraded birth. Here is the Kural for it:

> Possession of good conduct is true nobility;  
> misconduct makes one's birth low.

Chapter 14 — Good Conduct; Kural 133
