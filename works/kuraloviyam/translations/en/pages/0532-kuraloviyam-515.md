In the story of Kannan, is there not a scene in which Vasudevan undertakes a long midnight journey to leave the eighth child born to him and Devaki in the cowherd settlement, so that the child may grow there in safety and abundance?

Just so, clasping his baby brother to his chest, this man continues his journey through that terrifying darkness.

A few people who had started with him as companions turned back on the road the moment they saw the first drizzle.

Friends who had spoken sweetly—

“We are friends dearer than life! Loving brothers!”

—friends who had walked step for step with him, saw the heavy thunder, rain and storm wind halfway through the journey and ran back as fast as they could.

The more he thought of them, the more he laughed.

“People who have learned only to taste pleasure are afraid even to touch suffering. Such people can never truly be close to anyone.”

his lips murmured.

More than the pain inflicted by nature's fury, what tormented him was the hurt caused by people who had been sweet companions in comfort but turned their faces away when hardship came.

Even so, determined to gain the joy of fulfilling his duty and protecting the child, he marched bravely against nature's assault.

At last he succeeded.

Looking at this hero of duty, Valluvar praises him in this way:

“There is no greatness higher than the greatness of one who undertakes a rightful duty without saying, ‘I will grow weary and withdraw my hand.’”

Here is the Kural:

> No greatness stands above the greatness of one  
> who says, “I will not withdraw my hand from doing my duty.”

Chapter 103 — The Work of Sustaining One's House and Line; Kural 1021

கைதூவேன் = I will not grow weary and withdraw
