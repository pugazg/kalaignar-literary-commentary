"You sinner! Would you not even wave away a crow with the hand from which you have eaten?" the poor man groaned.

"Yes, yes! Why should I chase a crow away with that hand? Instead of letting a grain fall from my hand for the crow, is it a crime if I eat it myself?"

"What a miser!"

The moment the poor man uttered that word, Anbukkadal sprang upon him.

The body that was already dying collapsed before it was fully dead.

Then something happened like lightning.

Before Anbukkadal stood a brute who looked even stronger than he was.

The brute's long arms bent.

Those bent arms played across Anbukkadal's cheek with the speed of a storm.

He cried out and fell.

The brute seized the diamond-filled bag from his shoulder.

Anbukkadal fought to recover it, but failed.

He chased the brute and kept running.

Far away, the man disappeared.

Meanwhile crows and sparrows ate their fill from the food Anbukkadal had abandoned.

The poor man, curled upon the ground and close to death, seemed to mutter something.

Listening closely, one heard a Kural:

> The base will not even shake food from a moist hand for another,  
> except for those whose bent fist can break their cheek.

"The miserly and base will not shake even their damp hand for the needy; they yield only to one stronger than themselves, whose bent hand can smash their cheek."

That is the meaning of the Kural.

> When the poor who run become those who strike,  
> and those who run and those who strike together  
> become equals in a single moment—understand this!

So sang Paavendar Bharathidasan.

How beautifully, two thousand years earlier, Valluvar had given that thought within two lines!
