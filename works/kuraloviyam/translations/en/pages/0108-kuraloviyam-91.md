## Visual material

Colour illustration above the prose showing a young woman seated near a cooking fire, with tally-like marks visible on the wall behind her.

Are her brows the Chera bow? Are her eyes the warrior's arrows? Is this magnetic statue a feast for thought? This unwritten picture of a woman is neither before the mirror that displays beauty in a mansion of letters, nor amid an evening garden rich in nature's loveliness. She is seated in the kitchen. Why? The question insists on being asked.

That doll of silk-like beauty is preparing pittu for her lover. The parents who had raised her, salting her food, gave her to him, comparing her to a flower. But he took up a sharp sword and went away to strike down the growing enemy. Though she begged him with folded hands, he would not stay. The days moved and vanished. She turns the pages of her heart. She counts the lines on the long wall. They are not tally marks for milk or curd. They are lines of attachment—the count of the days he has been away from her. Looking at the lines, she sighs deeply. Will he come today? Will he bring joy, or not? Will tonight too be torment? So yearns the great beauty with breasts like lotus buds. She aches because the handsome one has not come to eat the pittu she has prepared for him. And that is not all. Mischievous Valluvar says that by touching and counting those picture-like lines again and again—the marks that tell how many days he has been separated from her—even her fingers have worn away. Here is the Kural:

> Watching the way, her eyes have lost their brightness;  
> counting the days he went, her fingers have worn thin.

Chapter 127 — Longing for His Return; Kural 1261

The meaning is: looking and looking along the road, her eyes have dimmed; counting the day-marks, her fingers too have worn away.
