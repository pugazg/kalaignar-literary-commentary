...the old woman—the "brown leaf"—would stay in the hut, and Kanmaniyaal would take over that task.

Kathiroli had carved so many statues of beautiful women, yet he had never been able to carve one like her.

He marvelled.

He marvelled.

Both grew afraid and kept apart.

But the old woman had noticed the language spoken by Kathiroli's eyes and Kanmaniyaal's eyes.

So she began guarding the girl closely.

Is it not when obstacles appear that love gains new strength from the urge to overcome them?

Kathiroli longed somehow to meet her one day, speak freely, and become companions in life—partners in spring.

Because of that longing, he even began leaving some sculptures half-finished.

One day he wrote a short note.

That night, no matter what the hour, she must come and meet him.

He could not go there; her mother would be present.

So he wrote to the good maiden who had captured his heart:

"Kanmani! Tonight, whatever the hour, the door of my hut will remain unbolted. I will wait for you even until dawn."

When she came carrying food with the old woman, he somehow pressed the love-note into her hand.

She lay beside her mother pretending to sleep.

Kathiroli's hut was nearby.

Still, how could she deceive her mother and leave?

A great struggle in her heart.

Outside there was thunder and lightning.

Rain poured.

The sea roared.

In that noise, if she opened the door, the sound would not reach the old woman's ears.

She gathered courage.

She rose.

As she walked toward Kathiroli's hut, the rain bathed her.

Her wet clothes clung to her body and revealed her beauty.

She touched the door of Kathiroli's hut.

It opened.

Those doors had been waiting.

There was no speech.

He embraced her completely.

Her body, chilled by rain, now grew warm beneath his lips.

At the very moment the two stood intoxicated by delight, Ponniyammai entered through the shivering cold.

She gripped Kanmaniyaal's arms tightly and dragged her away.

Kathiroli stood motionless.

Only his lips moved slowly.
