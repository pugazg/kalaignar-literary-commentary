Then a mischievous young man approaches him carrying a cup and says humbly, “King of kings! You are very tired. Please drink a little milk.” The madman takes the cup and gulps down everything in it. What the young man called it was milk—but what was in the cup was liquor! What happens if a man who is already mad drinks intoxicating liquor as well? This madness joins the madness already upon him. With whom does Valluvar compare that man?

A fool, it seems, gets some possession into his hands. Do you know what happens to him once he obtains it? He becomes like a madman who has also drunk liquor!

Here is a Kural for this explanation:

> When something comes into a fool's possession,  
> it is like a madman becoming drunk.

Kuralar goes on to praise friendship with such a degraded fool as something exceedingly sweet.

Is that not strange? A man who cannot distinguish good from bad, who does not walk the path of conduct, who becomes like a madman drunk on liquor the moment some possession falls into his hands—yet his friendship is said to be exceedingly sweet? What kind of jest is this!

“Great indeed is the friendship of fools...” Yes! The friendship of fools is said to be greatly sweet.

Why? How?

> ...for when it ends,  
> it brings not a single pain.

When such friendship is broken, no sorrow arises. If friendship with a fool withers away, the heart is spared anguish. Therefore the friendship of a fool is sweet! If friendship with the wise, the capable, the good and the accomplished comes to harm, it pains the heart. If friendship with fools disappears, there is no disturbance within. Thus friendship with a fool is excellent.

The more one reflects on how this Kural paints so great a truth as an indirect picture of humour, does not the heart swell with joy?

> Great indeed is the friendship of fools; when parting comes,  
> it brings not a single pain.
