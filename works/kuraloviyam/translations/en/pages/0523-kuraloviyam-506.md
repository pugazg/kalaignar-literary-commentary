The king whom he had once served as commander was shocked.

Yet because that king still possessed commanders and soldiers rich in firmness, gratitude and patriotism, he continued the war.

Meanwhile, the surrendered commander, who had lain at the enemy king's feet without receiving a post, at last obtained a small position.

Praise! Salutations! Speeches of admiration in colourful Tamil!

He began comparing the enemy king who gave him the post to a god.

His wife and his own son condemned his conduct.

They told him sharply that it would be far more honourable to remain with their own king and receive a glorious wound on the battlefield than to accept office in the enemy king's palace and loudly praise a cruel man as lord and noble ruler.

Townspeople, relatives, friends, even those in the enemy king's court—none were willing to respect him.

One day he entered the house with his head hanging.

His wife asked why his face was so fallen.

“I thought the post was everything,” he said with tears in his eyes. “But I am ashamed to hear what people say about me both openly and behind my back.”

At that moment his son came in, making a wooden puppet dance by pulling its string.

“What is this—a wooden doll?” asked the mother.

“Mother! It is alive. Look!”

He tugged the string and made it move.

The man understood the mockery his wife and son were directing at him.

Valluvar had written a Kural saying:

“The movement of those who carry no shame within their hearts is like a wooden puppet tied to a string and made to deceive others as though it were alive.”

He understood that his wife and son were holding that Kural up against his shameless conduct.

His mind burned with humiliation.

> The movement of those who have no shame within  
> is like a wooden puppet whose string deceives us into thinking it alive.
