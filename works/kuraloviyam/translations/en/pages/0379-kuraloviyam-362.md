She placed appam before her beloved, who had come and seated himself in the hall. Delighting in the beauty of his fingers touching it and the way he slowly swallowed it, she sent him off to work, then stood motionless in the house like a pillar, her eyes following the path he had taken.

The morning hours kept her company as she savoured again in memory all the pleasures of the night and swelled with joy.

After that her duties continued. She sent a packed meal for her husband to eat in the afternoon while he was away at work.

Then evening came. The peacock who had danced in joy that morning stood troubled at the sight of evening. Why? For what reason? He would return only at night. That was what he had said before leaving. Meanwhile the sickness of love, budding in the morning and flowering in the evening, had seized her. What could she do? She writhed under the cruelty of the illness. Evening tormented and exhausted her. She felt compelled to compare morning and evening.

“What good have I done to the morning? And what hostile wrong have I done to this evening?”

she asked herself in anguish.

The following Kural reveals the meaning of her question:

> What good have I done to morning, and what enmity  
> have I shown evening, that it treats me so?

Chapter 123 — Lamenting at the Sight of Evening; Kural 1225
