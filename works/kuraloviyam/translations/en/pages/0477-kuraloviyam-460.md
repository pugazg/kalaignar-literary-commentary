He listened to her without moving.

She would not let go. She pulled him along and set out to see the poet, his father.

The two stood before him.

The poet was seated on a torn mat beside the cold, sleeping stove, inscribing poems on old palm leaves.

Seeing the two of them, he was overcome with surprise and asked his son:

“Who is this girl?”

“Father—”

That was all the young poet's lips managed to say.

Before he could go further, the bold young woman said:

“We love each other with all our hearts. We came to receive your blessing and marry—”

She did not finish.

The poet's face exploded with rage.

“We do not have even a quarter-coin to our name! There is no one left who will lend us money! And now love is flourishing—is it? Love! You fool, what farce is this? Where did you pick up this little whore?”

The words burst from him in a torrent.

The green parrot who had brought the young man there without fear covered her ears crying, “Alas!” and ran away, not knowing where she was going.

He flew after her.

A great river rushed and swirled nearby. Before she could be caught in the current and die, he stopped her and lifted her away.

Slowly her red lips moved.

“I thought you were born into a good family. I thought the same of your father. And he looked at me and spoke such degrading words! No—not words of degradation. Words like thunder!”

She sobbed and sobbed.

He held her tightly. Upon the beautiful eyelids that had closed in the greatness of that embrace, he pressed his lips gently.

“My cuckoo! Have you never read the Kural? How movingly it says that the cruelty called poverty can create, even in people born to good families, the weariness from which degrading words arise. Is my father an exception to that? So do not take that degrading expression to heart.”

She found comfort not only in his lap, but in his explanation as well.

There on the riverbank they held their betrothal. Though they had no betel leaves to exchange, their four tender lips became the betel leaves of the ceremony.
