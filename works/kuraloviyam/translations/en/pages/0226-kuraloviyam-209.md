She is about to sneeze. But the sneeze that came upon her suddenly subsides. She cannot bear that sorrow.

“Oh! It showed the sign of a sneeze coming, yet disappeared without arriving! What could be the reason? Perhaps my lover seemed about to think of me, but then stopped thinking of me?”

So the beautiful woman broods in distress.

> Does he only seem to think of me, yet not truly think?  
> The sneeze buds as though to come, and then dies away.

Here `சினைத்தல்` means “to bud.” Because the sneeze perished while still in the budding stage—because the lover forgot the thought while still in the stage of thinking!

Another chamber of love! There the lover has a fear. What is it? A sneeze is coming upon him. He struggles to suppress it somehow. If he sneezes, his beloved will immediately grow suspicious and ask, “Who was thinking of you?” That could lead to a lovers' quarrel—why invite such trouble? So he struggles to hold back the sneeze. The maiden sees this and understands. “Why are you suppressing your sneeze? Are you hiding from me that another woman is thinking of you?” she asks, and begins to cry.

> When I suppressed a sneeze, she wept and asked,  
> “Did you hide from me that someone dear to you was thinking of you?”

How hard must he have struggled to dispel that green-parrot maiden's suspicion?

To show how another young dove grieves, Valluvar uses the sneeze as a comparison in yet another sweet Kural:

“What can I do, my friend? I try in every possible way to hide within myself the desire I feel for him. But, refusing to remain hidden according to my wish, it reveals itself of its own accord like a sneeze.”

How aptly that lord among honeyed-Tamil poets paints “sneezing” as the image for desire that cannot be concealed!

> I would hide my desire; yet, without heeding my intent,  
> it appears of itself like a sneeze.
