Shock among those gathered in the hall! A thunderclap of silence! A flood of tears! The man who had stood on the stage praising him was nowhere to be seen.

The poet rose and walked out of the hall. His friend followed him.

Nearby stood a small pavilion. There, the man who had offered all that praise had gathered four people around him and was talking. Hidden from sight, the poet and his friend listened.

“What does this fellow know? I thought it even then—how could they take him and make him a court poet! He's a wanderer! Is that writing? It's fate written out! Is that speech? It's the howl of a ghost! His brain belongs in a furnace. The king has done a good thing!”

The poet's friend could not bear it. The poet calmed him.

“Alas! This is unbearable suffering! Let me go. I will tear him to pieces!” the friend cried, springing up.

The poet explained:

“My friend! Why do you regard this as suffering? A little while ago this same man praised me; now he insults me. That is all, is it not? Only if his praise had given me pleasure would his insult now give me pain! If, when happiness comes, one does not desire, exalt and celebrate it, then when suffering comes there is no need to experience the anguish of that suffering. That is why, my friend, Valluvar says:

> One who does not crave delight when delight comes  
> does not suffer pain as pain when sorrow comes.

That is what he says! Therefore, if we can bear happiness, we can bear suffering too. If we wander intoxicated by happiness, we cannot turn suffering away.

I regard his praise then and his insult now as one and the same. Come, let us go!”

The poet and his friend left that place.
