—among the flowered creepers, the cuckoos sat pouring out background music for the lovers' play of delight. The tender shoots of the trees swayed in the soft breeze, fanning away the pearl-like beads of sweat that appeared on the brows of the lovers who enjoyed the pleasure of union beneath them as though under a parasol. The fallen dry leaves rustled in a flood of joy, as though rejoicing in the great fortune of becoming a soft bed for them.

The dance of pleasure came sweetly to completion, with the jingling of two pairs of bangles as the nattuvangam and four lips as the accompanying instruments—must anything more be described?

For those who have tasted that delicious segment of fruit called pleasure, there is also the great medicine called “lovers' quarrel” to give still more flavour. I call something that gives flavour a medicine! No, no—Valluvar says so.

A man keeps eating a six-flavoured meal. If he continues eating without pause, can he experience the taste of the food he has consumed? He cannot. Then what must he do? He must allow an interval for the food to digest. Valluvar observes that “the digestion of what has already been eaten is sweeter than eating.” Is that not, in one way, a medicine suited to the body? In the same way, it is said that there is greater pleasure in the lovers' quarrel that follows union than in the delight lovers gain by joining together. Is it not best first to eat, allow it to digest, and then begin eating again when hunger returns?

Here is the Kural for it:

> Sweeter than eating is the digestion of what was eaten;  
> sweeter than union in love is lovers' quarrel.

Chapter 133 — Joy of Lovers' Quarrel; Kural 1326
