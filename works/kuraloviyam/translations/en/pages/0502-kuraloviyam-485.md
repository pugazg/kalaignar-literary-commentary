—and looked at the rich youth in astonishment.

“Ponnaiya! What is this? Why are you standing there holding a rat?”

Ponnaiya was the name of the wealthy family's son.

“Hey, Chinnappa! How dare you call me by my name? Have you grown that insolent?”

Ponnaiya scolded Chinnappa, the poor young man.

“All right, all right. Forgive me, sir. Tell me why you have brought the rat.”

“This is a dead rat. I came to throw it into this tank as food for the fish.”

Saying this, he flung the rat into the water.

“Good heavens! This is a public tank. Is it not enough that you yourself are unclean? Must you foul and ruin the tank too?”

Ponnaiya could not tolerate Chinnappa's words.

“What did you say, fool? Who is unclean—you or I? Dirt is on your body; that is why you bathe. I never bathe at all, because there is not even a mustard-seed's worth of dirt on my body!”

he shouted, and walked away carrying a mountain of filth upon himself.

Chinnappa stood still for a moment.

From far away he heard someone singing a Kural:

> If one could see one's own fault as one sees an enemy's fault,  
> what evil could remain in life?

If people were able to see their own faults just as clearly as they see the faults of others, Valluvar asks, what evil would remain in life?

Ponnaiya had called Chinnappa's bathing itself a fault, pointing to it as proof that dirt existed on Chinnappa's body.

Yet Ponnaiya himself wandered about like a vessel full of filth.

Chinnappa was insulted for speaking well.

Even if Ponnaiya's conduct and lack of civility did not show their effects immediately, they would surely bring harm, illness and suffering into his life in the future.

Smiling, Chinnappa climbed out of the tank.
