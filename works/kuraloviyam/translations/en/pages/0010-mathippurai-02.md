Thus we see Valluvar moving through *Kuraloviyam*. Just as Kambar is said to have heard variant readings of his *Ramayanam* in his own lifetime, Kalaignar has fashioned a background in which Valluvar himself hears an interpretation unsuited to the Kural beginning “Do not say, ‘this is the path of virtue’,” and corrects it with the true meaning. This is scholarship joined with courage.

In my book *Valluvam*, I have stressed that the Thirukkural is a mode of life suited to each person, a practical book corresponding to one’s own conduct, and that the benefit of the Kural lies in each person setting it beside the course of his or her own life. Kalaignar, whose view accords with mine, has woven Kurals from all three divisions into the practical lives of many kinds of people. I wish to point out that this is a new method, one not previously seen in books written about the Thirukkural, but one that ought to be seen.

It is inappropriate to explain the wise Thirukkural through the heavenly lives of gods or the bewildering tales of mythology. The conduct of good human beings who lived as we do is what properly serves as an illustration for the Kural. For the Kural phrase “even within worshipping hands a weapon may be concealed,” our Kalaignar points to the shooting of Gandhi; for “though poison is seen, they drink it,” to Bhutto’s hanging in Pakistan; for “the greatness of those whose words are fulfilled,” to Perarignar Anna’s moral counsel; for “the friendship of those full as brimming water,” to the friendship between Periyar and the elder statesman; and for “she who is worthy as a life-companion,” to the greatness of Jenny, wife of Karl Marx. Because it shows what has actually happened, the Thirukkural appears as a book of life; because it also draws upon the lives of good and learned people of other lands, it appears as a universal ethical text. These qualities stand out like the *thaali* upon the neck of a woman of steadfast virtue.

Within such a background Kalaignar also reveals an incident from his own family. Hearing it makes the body thrill and tremble. His beloved father Muthuvelar, realizing that a cobra was crawling over him, remained without trembling in heart or moving his body until the snake that had climbed upon him went down of its own accord, and thus escaped its bite. *Kuraloviyam* gives this true incident as an illustration for the Kural phrase “suffering as vast as a flood.” It is a household example of the many places in which Kalaignar sees a Kural-scene. In the accompanying picture, Mother Anjugam is shown in the background watching without moving; this reveals the dignity of the household into which Kalaignar was born.
