The king who had come hunting vowed, “I will not return without hunting this doe,” and went back to his lodging.

Before dawn he disguised himself as the sage.

The real sage had risen before daybreak and gone to bathe in the river and worship the Lord, keeping to his morning discipline.

Without letting the disguise slip and without letting his lust cool, Indran entered the hut.

He drank deeply from the sleeping beauty and melted in her embrace.

Believing him to be the sage she had married, she satisfied a long-standing longing.

Even after she realized midway that he was not her husband, she lacked the power to restrain the senses and remained intoxicated by pleasure.

The sage returned after worship and raged like a storm. He leapt like a tiger.

“Plotters! Wretches!”

he cursed.

Ahalikai stood like stone, unable to answer her husband.

Indran wept in anguish, his eyes turned into wounds, thinking of the error born from his failure to restrain the senses.

This, the Kural says, is the point:

Indran stands as evidence of a man who loses his way because he cannot restrain his senses, and thereby demonstrates to the world the strength of the person who can cast away the desires produced by the five senses.

Here is the verse:

> The power of one who has subdued the five senses—  
> Indran, king of the vast heavens, is proof enough.

Chapter 3 — The Greatness of Renunciates; Kural 25
