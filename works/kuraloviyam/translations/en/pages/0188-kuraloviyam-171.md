—suffering, stood before her as the maiden called Evening. The lover-separated woman looked at her. Evening has no light of its own, does it?

Evening is the time when day and night mingle before one another and stand there in a state of dim confusion.

She asked that lightless evening-woman:

“Why are you bewildered and troubled? I am the one who, separated from him and unable to bear the pain, looks like a creeper that has withered away. What has happened to you?”

The evening-woman gave no answer. Her face began to fade still further.

The maiden would not let the matter go. She herself supplied the answer the woman Evening ought to have given.

“Could it be that you too are suffering like me?”

The evening-woman gently moved her lips and let sorrow-threaded words fall:

“From morning until now I danced in delight with a body full of light. My lover, the sun who is like my very eye, kept me tightly embraced. While I had lost myself in that pleasure, he slowly loosened his arms, laid me down upon the earth and went away toward the western direction. Would I not suffer? You tell me!”

Saying this, the evening-maiden placed a hand upon the woman's shoulder and leaned against her. Stroking Evening's hair, the lover-separated woman said:

“O bewildered woman Evening! I had thought only my companion was merciless, because he left me and robbed me of my light. Is your companion too as lacking in mercy as my lover?”

Those words seemed to give a little comfort to the dimmed evening-woman.

The two who suffer from separation console one another. Yet by calling their companions merciless, are they not also revealing the love for them that cannot be exhausted? And more than that, evening itself causes this woman pain, so it is said that evening too has no happiness. Therefore, with a cry of anguish, she blesses it: “Live on!” Here is the Kural:

> Live on, sorrow-dimmed, bewildered evening! Is your companion too,  
> like mine, hard-hearted?
