Nannan looked closely at Uthiyan, who lay unconscious. He knew that the green-stoned ring and the red-stoned ring glittering on Uthiyan's fingers were costly. Before the servant returned, Nannan dared to try removing them. He began to pull at them. Uthiyan groaned. Nannan immediately became the picture of a good man.

By then the servant arrived with the physician. They carried Uthiyan to the physician's house. After a short treatment, Uthiyan opened his eyes. The physician happily announced that his life had been saved.

Later Uthiyan, Nannan and the servant returned to Uthiyan's house. Nannan embraced Uthiyan, offered his good wishes, and went home.

A few naazhigai after he left, a letter arrived for him.

"Dear Nannan,

"My servant told me many times how false your friendship toward me was, but I would not listen. Everything that happened yesterday evening was arranged according to my servant's plan. The news that my ships had sunk at sea was false. My chest pain and fainting were false. But your attempt to remove my costly rings—that was true.

"Following the Kural's teaching that one must abandon the friendship of those whose character does not match one's own, whatever the price, I am ending your friendship from today by giving you the rings you desired as a gift.

"Compared with your friendship, the friendship of my servant who seeks my welfare is this: you are a pit; he is a mountain. It is the friendship of people like him that I seek.

"Greetings,
Uthiyan."

> Cultivate the friendship of the blameless; even by giving something up,  
> abandon the friendship of those who are unworthy of such fellowship.

Chapter 80 — Examining Friendship; Kural 800
