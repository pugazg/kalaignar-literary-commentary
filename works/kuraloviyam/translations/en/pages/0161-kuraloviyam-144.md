A warrior comes bearing a spear. The elephant confronts him. The warrior too fights bravely to defeat the elephant. Is he not a Tamil? Does he not belong to a tradition that never turns its back?

With chest held high and spear raised, he charges at the elephant. The elephant, in turn, cunningly extends its tusks, pierces the warrior's stomach and tosses him upward. At the threshold of death the spear-bearer lets out a cry of pain—a cry still mingled with valour. With the force of having gored the warrior, the elephant strides in triumph, running here and there in rage and fury. As it runs, it places a foot in a muddy pool that has collected beside a rock. It cannot pull the foot out. The elephant is a heavy animal. Its weight sinks toward the earth, and there is no support strong enough to help it rise and reach the bank. The elephant struggles. Then something curious happens. What is it?

A jackal notices the elephant's condition. Deciding that this is the moment, the jackal leaps upon the elephant and finds a way to feed itself. With teeth and claws it tears at the elephant's body, drinks its blood and eats its flesh. The elephant, unable to fight back, groans and writhes, unable to bear the pain. Had it not been trapped in that mud, the jackal would have been reduced to pulp beneath a single stamp of its foot. But now the jackal has a feast! Even if it roamed the forest all day, it would never find food like this.

Through a beautiful picture in Kural form, Valluvar shows the idea that an elephant strong enough to fling a warrior about may, if trapped in mud, be defeated even by a jackal. The Puranic storytellers used an elephant to tell the unbelievable fantasy called Gajendra Moksha. Valluvar, however, uses an elephant to paint advice needed by a government.

However great its military strength, because of a small weakness a state may be seized by enemies without strength. To make this point, Valluvar paints in Kuraloviyam the strength of the elephant, the news that in the mud its strength became worthless, and the wonder of a little jackal defeating it.

Even the greatest thinkers and warriors celebrated throughout the world have been brought down by smaller men because of small mistakes.

Was it not because the great warrior Shivaji, given by Maharashtra, set foot in the mud of superstition that he knelt before the Vaidika leader Kakabhattar? And did not Caesar and Antony, lamps of world history, fail to preserve their society and come to ruin because they became trapped in the mud of pleasure offered by the beautiful Cleopatra?

Here, then, is the Kural wisdom our Tamil leader gave to every government that rules a country! The elephant that gored and killed a spear-bearing warrior can, if trapped in mud, be opposed and destroyed even by a jackal.

> An elephant that faces spear-bearing men without fear  
> may, with its feet sunk in deep mire, be felled by a jackal.
