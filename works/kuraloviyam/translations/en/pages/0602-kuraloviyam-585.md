Vessel: O lid, a part of my own body! Everyone thinks that the two of us are joined together, don't they?

Lid: Yes. What doubt is there? Am I not always here without leaving you?

Vessel: That is what everyone thinks—that you never leave. But when others come to take what is inside me, you move away from me and assist them. Taking advantage of your action, they take away what I hold within.

Lid: What can I do about that? I am joined to you all the time! Even those who use an example for close union praise it by saying, "like a vessel and its lid."

Vessel: True. That is how it appears from outside. But because there is no firm bond between us, others make you move away from me and take what is inside. Lid! Do you know what this resembles? It is as unstable as the unity between the elder and younger brother in this house. By exploiting the younger brother's differences with the elder, enemies try to separate him from his brother and accomplish their own purposes. It is just like that.

Irangai awoke with a start. The dream vanished.

The comparison Koventhako had made that morning—"the vessel and its lid"—returned to her mind.

With that thought rising again, she left the kitchen and entered the bedroom. There her husband Ezhilko was reading the Thirukkural.

"Which Kural are you studying today?" she asked.

"Do you know what a beautiful comparison Valluvar has given for internal enmity?" he asked in wonder.

"Tell me too."

"I will. A lid may appear from outside to fit closely upon the small container called a seppu. Yet it can move away, leaving the container open so that others may use what is inside. In the same way, internal enmity may look like outward union, but inwardly there is no real joining."

He first explained the meaning and then read the Kural:

> Though joined like a lid fitted upon its vessel,  
> a household touched by internal enmity is never truly joined.
