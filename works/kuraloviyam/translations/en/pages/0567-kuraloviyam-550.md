...rolling over their laps and backs. A nursing infant was at the woman's breast—temporarily, perhaps, the youngest child.

Paavai looked at the scene and then at Pagalavan.

Pagalavan spoke mockingly. Both had studied Tamil literature, so he said it with confidence that Paavai would understand at once.

"Valluvar has written that the touch of one's children gives delight to the whole body, and hearing their prattle gives delight to the ear. This must be what that scene means."

"Dear! Just because Valluvar said 'children,' must an entire procession of children lay siege to their parents and give pleasure by touching their bodies? Or must they shout and give delight to the ear? Look at those parents' faces! What weariness! What irritation! If there were only two children touching them and speaking their baby words, then even in this poor household there really would be joy overflowing!"

Pagalavan did not hesitate to answer Paavai.

"Then why didn't Valluvar say, 'Have children in measure and live in prosperity'? He could have stated in the Kural that two are enough, and that itself is happiness, couldn't he?" he said, pouring out a teasing smile.

"Dear! Through the Kural that says 'the flute is sweet, the yaazh is sweet,' why should we not understand that Valluvar was speaking of only two children?"

"How? How?"

"Yes, dear! The flute is generally an instrument associated with men, while the yaazh is mostly played by women. We can say that, like those two instruments, one boy whose baby voice defeats the flute and one girl whose baby speech defeats the yaazh are enough, can't we? If Valluvar had thought that the simultaneous shouting of many children must split one's ears and that this was sweetness, instead of naming the two instruments flute and yaazh, would he not have described it with the idea that 'an orchestra is sweet'?"

Pagalavan marvelled at Paavai's cleverness. Overflowing with happiness, the two approached the park. Before long they returned home. Their ten-year-old son, who had been sitting on the veranda studying, ran up shouting, "Appa! Amma!" and embraced them.

"Come, come! The music of the flute has lost before the music of your voice!" said Pagalavan.

Hearing the sound, their five-year-old daughter ran in.

"What is it, my dear? You have defeated the yaazh!" he said, embracing her. The two children clasped their parents tightly.

> hearing their words is delight to the ear.
