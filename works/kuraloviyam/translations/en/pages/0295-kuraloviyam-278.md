She would sorrowfully stroke the hands that embraced her, hardened from gripping an axe and cutting wood, and press her lips against those palms as though giving them relief.

“Do my rough hands trouble you?” he would ask his peacock-like beloved. “No, no! It is precisely in your roughness that all the weariness of my day's labour disappears,” she would reply, and the two would embrace in overflowing joy.

One day there was a full moon. Both wished to enjoy themselves in its light. A mango tree stood before their little house. They spread a mat in the shelter of its shade and lay drinking in the moon's beautiful light. “Look, even that moon pushes aside the cloud that covers it,” he said, pushing aside the cloth over her breasts. Beneath the mango tree they composed an epic of love.

The day after that night of delight, he left her and went to another town. It was only a one-day separation. Yet she sat beneath the mango tree waiting for his return. She did not eat, sleep would not come, and the day itself would not pass.

That night came to her like another daytime. Even then she sat beneath the tree. The moon appeared and seemed to read again and again the pages of the literature of pleasure they had written beneath that tree the previous night.

She became motionless. Without realizing it, she leaned against the trunk. Her eyes remained fixed. Time passed like this.

After a long while he returned. Instead of coming from the front, he came from behind, rested his head on her shoulder and pressed his lips to her neck. Startled, she sprang up.

“For all these naazhigai, where had you surrendered your heart? To the beauty of the moon?” he asked.

“No, no! My heart, distressed because it could not see you, had climbed onto this branch so that it could look far away and see whether you were coming. Only now has it come down from the branch.”

Saying this, she embraced him and wept.

> Thinking of the return of the lover from whom I parted after union,  
> my heart climbs a branch.
