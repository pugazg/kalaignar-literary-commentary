He was a man of such fine skill. As he walked along the forest path, he was also thinking about the further work he ought to undertake in the field of medicine.

Then, from somewhere, came a growling sound! He did not tremble in fear. He turned his ear toward the direction of the sound and listened closely. The growl was not mixed with anger or frenzy; it was mixed with pain. Even so, the physician understood that it was the sound of a fierce animal.

The force of the sound too was gradually weakening. The physician decided that, whatever it might be, he would go near and see. He walked toward the sound. It was indeed a cry of pain—but the cry of a tiger. The physician looked at the beast with eyes full of compassion. The tiger lay fallen on the ground. A sharp arrow had pierced its paw. The physician understood what had happened. Some hunter had drawn his bow at the tiger. Seeing him, the tiger had tried to spring upon him. Before it could do so, the hunter's arrow had struck its paw. Wounded by the arrow, the tiger had run in terrible pain. The arrowhead had therefore sunk deep into its paw and the tiger could no longer walk. Unable to bear the pain, it had collapsed.

The good-hearted physician did not delay even for a moment. He went near the tiger and stroked its back. He patted its head. Perhaps that act gave the tiger some comfort; it lay quietly. Then the physician crushed and squeezed the juice from some leaves he carried and applied it to the tiger's wounded paw. The juice helped numb the pain enough for him to pull the arrow from the paw. He threw the arrow far away and applied medicine to the wound. The tiger rose to its feet.

The physician too stood up with peace of mind, consoled that in his life he had managed to help even a fierce beast.

Suddenly a terrifying growl rang out. The physician started. That very tiger sprang upon him. It tore open his chest, severed his arms and legs, crushed his head, and satisfied its hunger. The tiger that only a little while earlier had been struggling for its life now delighted in taking the life of the man who had saved it.

We have read the song, “The healer who cured the striped tiger's illness,” have we not? The truth explained by that song is the very truth Thiruvalluvar proclaimed in Kural form two thousand years ago.

Unless one understands each person's nature and acts in a way suited to each, error can arise even in doing good.

> Even in doing good there may be error,  
> if one acts without knowing each person's nature.

Chapter 47 — Acting After Due Consideration; Kural 469
