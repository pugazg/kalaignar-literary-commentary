> Without thinking of the rare sweetness  
> of the small-leaved gooseberry fruit,  
> you kept its power within your heart  
> and gave it to me, that death might depart.

In other words, Athiyaman Neduman Anji, ruler of Tagadur, obtained a rare gooseberry said to possess the power to grant a very long life.

Just as he was about to eat that precious fruit, a medicine against death, news came that a guest had arrived outside.

Who was the guest?

Avvai herself.

Then Athiyaman's broad heart began to speak.

What did it say?

“It is not fitting to leave a guest outside and eat alone—even if what one eats is a medicine that conquers death.”

The rule of hospitality says one should not eat alone but share food with the guest who has arrived.

Yet this guest was one who served a feast of Tamil itself.

So Athiyaman thought:

“If I eat this gooseberry and live long, that benefits only me. If Avvai eats it, Tamil itself will benefit.”

And he gave the fruit to Avvai.

Going a step beyond even the virtue of shared hospitality, Athiyaman gained an imperishable place in Sangam literature.

Yes—the man who gave Avvai the gooseberry as a medicine against death now lives forever through the deathless medicine of literature.

Here is the Kural on the excellence of hospitality:

> Even if the food were a medicine against death,  
> it is not fitting to eat while a guest remains outside.

Chapter 9 — Hospitality; Kural 82
