“Who is this fellow? Whenever we see him, he keeps wandering by lake-banks, pond-banks and mountain rocks. He looks at the sky. He looks at the earth. He furrows his brow. Who is he?”

When one asked like this, the other gave his explanation.

“They say he is Valluvar! And not just an ordinary Valluvar—Thiruvalluvar, it seems! They say he thinks! They say the madman is writing a book of virtue! Of all the lunatics, he is one more!”

Valluvar's thought was broken. The abusive words they had poured out—their words of contempt—fell upon his ears. He turned and looked at them. Still mocking Valluvar, they walked away. Along the lake-shore, several people were engaged in field work. In one place they were cutting into the earth. In another they were ploughing the fields. Elsewhere they were digging a well. Seeing all this, Valluvar found a beautiful Kural-image.

“The earth bears even those who cut and dig into her and wound her, and still supports them upon herself, does it not? In the same way, bearing with those who speak contemptuously of us is our foremost virtue,” Valluvar told himself. The Kural had been born.

> As the earth bears those who dig into it,  
> foremost is to bear with those who revile us.

Having written this Kural in his heart, Valluvar looked at the maiden Earth and shot a question at her.

“Maiden Earth, you endure and bear those who torment you! Looking at you, I too endured those who insulted me to my face. But I have a doubt. Maiden Earth, explain it to me. Leave aside those who utter blame to one's face: some people make it their habit to speak ill of a person when that person is absent. Do you consider it virtue to bear the weight of the bodies of such people too?”

That question of Valluvar's also takes shape as a Kural.

> Does the earth bear, for virtue's sake, the burden of one  
> who waits for another's absence and then speaks meanly of him?
