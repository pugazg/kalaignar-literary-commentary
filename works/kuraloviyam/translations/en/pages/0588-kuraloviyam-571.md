That leader went to meet the emperor who was living in hiding in the forest.

The emperor welcomed him warmly and expressed regret for having, under certain circumstances, governed without concern for the people.

The leader said to him:

"Your Majesty! Influenced by those around you, you supported injustice. Listening to the 'advice' of poisonous minds, you turned many good people into enemies. As a result you lost your flag and your sceptre. But those who overthrew you now leap at one another in fury, intent on mutual destruction, and they are not prepared to protect the welfare of the people."

The emperor, who had listened with his head bowed, took both the leader's hands and said with emotion:

"My friend! I know your steadfastness. Whether in friendship or enmity, you remain steadfast in your stand. Trust me. I regret the cruelties inflicted upon the people. If once again you stand with me on behalf of the people, I will regain the kingdom I lost, never forget my gratitude, honour your friendship forever, and protect the people."

The leader's heart melted. He promised to support the emperor and immediately announced that decision to the people of the country.

Because the citizens stood on his side, it became easy for the emperor to march against those who had seized his land. By then the minor rulers had themselves splintered through disunity. In the great war that followed, the emperor wore the garland of victory.

But the very people's leader who had laid the foundation for that victory—the one who had come of his own accord and added strength when the emperor lay hidden in a mountain cave—was the man to whom the emperor now forgot the promise he had made.

Seeing the leader's disappointment, one of the minor rulers asked him with both mockery and pity:

"Has clarity been born at least now? Do you understand the truth?"

"What can I do? There was no unity among you. That is what brought this consequence," replied the leader.

Then he added another explanation:

"Valluvar has said that only those who possess firm hearts without crookedness will also possess speech without crookedness. That ethical quality is called impartiality."

> Speech has no crookedness when, with firm resolve,  
> there is no crookedness within.

Chapter 12 — Impartiality; Kural 119
