While the learned elder was explaining matters in this way, a voice was heard: “What cruelty! You are digging a pit and burying my very Kural!” The two elders turned in astonishment. Valluvar stood there with tears in his eyes. The elders bowed to him.

Valluvar bowed in return and said, “I do not know who devised such an unsuitable interpretation for the Kural I wrote! Elders! The meaning you have given to my Kural by using the men who carry the palanquin and the man who rides in it is not correct. I did not mean that the man in the palanquin is one who performed virtuous deeds, or that the man carrying it committed evil deeds. That is contrary to reason. Those who walk the path of virtue continue their journey through life, taking both its pleasures and its sufferings lightly, like the man travelling in the palanquin. Those who surrender themselves to an evil path, however, are like the palanquin bearers: they find no peace even in pleasure and lack the mental maturity to endure suffering, so they regard life itself as a great burden. I used the rider and the bearer only as an analogy for the effect of virtuous conduct. I never said that, according to each person’s sins and merits, some become palanquin bearers and others become riders! Do not spread mistaken ideas among the people without understanding me and my Kural,” he pleaded with feeling.

The learned elder expressed regret to Valluvar for what he had done and said that he would correct himself from then on.

“It is not enough merely to correct yourself; correct the commentary too,” said Valluvar, taking leave of them. By then, the palanquin had travelled far away.

> Do not say, “This is the way of virtue,” by pointing  
> To the one who bears the palanquin and the one who rides in it.

Chapter 4 — The Insistence on Virtue; Kural 37
