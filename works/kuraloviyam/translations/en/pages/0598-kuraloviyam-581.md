The British, who came for trade and gradually encircled many regions of the Indian subcontinent, laboured hard to bring the South—and especially Tamil Nadu—under their domination.

It was not easy for them to defeat and enslave firm Tamil warriors who regarded death as though it were a bridal bed on which one might sport with a maiden. The Maruthu Pandiyars, rulers of Sivaganga, stood in that line of heroic leaders.

To defeat them, the British had to advance inch by inch. In many places British forces scattered and fled before the armies of the two Maruthus.

Even so, equipped with newer weapons of war and aided by palaiyakkarars and minor rulers among the Tamil people who were ready to betray their own, the British were approaching the height of victory.

As a result, Periya Maruthu had to move into the forest with his forces and wage war through concealment and sudden attack.

Karuthaan, that close companion, decided that the British could no longer be defeated. With the thought that it was enough if he saved himself, he betrayed Chinna Maruthu to the British.

The captured Chinna Maruthu and the defeated Periya Maruthu were hanged by the British.

Did Karuthaan, who betrayed them, escape? No. The British officers hanged and killed him too.

What can one say? Even now Karuthaan's statue stands at Kalaiyar Kovil as a mark of his betrayal. A statue that ought to have stood as an example of friendship instead stands as a symbol of treachery.

The Kural had already pointed to such conduct beautifully and condemned it:

"People of firm intelligence and unshaken clarity will not stoop to degrading acts even when trapped in distress from which escape seems impossible."

Here is the Kural with that meaning:

> Even in adversity, those of unwavering understanding  
> will not do deeds that bring disgrace.

Chapter 66 — Purity in Action; Kural 654
