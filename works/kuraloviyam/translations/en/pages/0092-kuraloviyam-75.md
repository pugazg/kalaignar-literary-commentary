“...a bad man; even his look is not right. He keeps circling around women and thereby shows what sort of man he is. Twisted moustache, rough body, a mind full of stupidity—in short, he is no good! Those wandering eyes and that grinning mouth are enough to show that he is a lustful fellow!”

Thus the townspeople freely wrote their judgments about the two men. Both were commanders newly arrived to supervise the soldiers who provided protection to the people of that region.

A week passed after the welcome ceremony. One night, the commander who had won praise for his handsome appearance walked stealthily through the street. The townspeople watched him closely. At the end of the street lived a married woman named Thenmozhi. Her husband was away. The commander entered her house to embrace her and seek pleasure.

At the moment when the flower was being crushed, the other commander burst inside. “Wretch! Lowest of men! If you value your life, run!” he roared. The villain trembled at the sight. The two fought. The first man died. The second came to bear the name of a good man.

“The commander who appeared upright committed a base act. The one who appeared lowly became noble through his superior deed,” the town now praised.

Valluvar says the same in two lines: “The arrow appears straight, yet performs a cruel deed; the yaazh appears curved, yet gives delight.” He then explains that, likewise, people should not be measured by their outward form but by their actions. Here is that incomparable Kural:

> The arrow is straight yet cruel; the yaazh is curved yet good—  
> so judge people by the kind of deeds they perform.

Chapter 28 — False Conduct; Kural 279
