My friend went on speaking to me like this—in private.

“Let that be. Does she love you?” I asked.

“I do not know! I have not yet even tried to understand her heart. But she comes and dances in my dreams; she stands in my memory and fills me with delight. If I close the doors of my eyelids, she comes before me and smiles. I simply do not know how I am ever to make her mine, friend!” He let out a long sigh of longing.

“All right! All right! Take up a kavadi!” I said.

“A kavadi? Me? If I carry a kavadi to my beloved's house, will the people in the street not mock me? They will burst into derisive laughter! Why are you telling me to take up a kavadi?” my friend asked, drawing his face tight.

“Here! This kavadi is right here. Bring it over beside me,” I said.

(It was the paar-kavadi that the traditionalists in my household had prepared for fulfilling a vow to Palani!)

When my friend brought the kavadi near, I began to explain:

“Friend! If you are to succeed in love, Valluvar himself says that you must take up the kavadi!”

“What? Did Valluvar tell us to carry a kavadi?”

“Yes! He told us to take up the kavadi and look closely at it!”

“What do you mean?”

“Listen, friend, to the words he speaks! I will tell you—listen! When love exists on only one side, for a man or a woman, it brings suffering. How should it be? Just as the ‘load’ on this kavadi is balanced equally on its two sides, love too must exist on both sides. One-sided love gives no pleasure! It causes suffering! Only love that blossoms on both sides can shower delight!”

My friend asked in wonder, “What Kural is that?” Here is that Kural:

> Love borne on one side is painful; like a kavadi,  
> it is sweet when the load rests on both sides.

Chapter 120 — The Agony of Solitary Love; Kural 1196
