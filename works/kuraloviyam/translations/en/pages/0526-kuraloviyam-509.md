The hard-hearted man arguing the case answered in this way.

“But there is no wound on the king's body.”

“He may have been strangled without leaving a wound.”

“Who strangled him?”

“This commander—Arulalan.”

The conspirators pointed at him.

“Who is the witness that it was Arulalan?”

At this question from the judge, a man walked through the court and stood forward.

“Here! I am the witness!”

Everyone assembled looked on in agitation.

He was none other than the man who, through Arulalan's recommendation, had been made a deputy commander.

“Wretch! You? You who touched Arulalan's feet and gained your livelihood through him—you have come to stand here as a traitor?”

The lips of those in court murmured like this.

He stood upright without the slightest visible shame.

Only outwardly.

Inside, his heart had collapsed.

“Arulalan! If you burn with anger at him, how can he ever prosper?”

an elder asked angrily.

“He has decided that he can live by any means. Poor man—let him somehow live and go his way.”

Arulalan replied.

In the end justice prevailed.

Those who had heaped false blame upon Arulalan left with heads hanging.

Unable even to look at Arulalan's face, the ungrateful man too departed from the court.

From somewhere, the words of a Kural sounded through music:

> There may be redemption even for one who destroys every other good;  
> there is none for the man who destroys gratitude for a good deed done.

Chapter 11 — Gratitude; Kural 110
