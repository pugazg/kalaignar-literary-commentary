This terrifying dance of nature may not happen every day, but from time to time it does.

Villages may be destroyed. Countries may be ruined.

Why, the whole world itself may be tossed about by the sea, hurled into fragments by violent storms and cyclones, shaken from its foundations by earthquakes, and thrown around as a disfigured ball of destruction.

The ancients called such devastation an *oozhi*—a world-ending catastrophe.

Sufferings, troubles and sorrows like such an oozhi can pursue a human being.

When they do, what does Valluvar call the person who remains unmoving and unshaken—who does not lose balance, whose heart does not panic, who does not wring his hands, who does not break down and weep, and who remains unchanged in principle no matter what changes arise around him?

He does not merely praise that act as courage or bravery and stop there.

By opening his heart in praise of people of principle who bear enormous suffering and remain firm without changing, he seems to discharge his anger at cowardly people who change even when they cannot bear small troubles.

How does he praise such firmness?

He calls it a quality.

What kind of quality?

An ocean of character.

“Even if the transformations of an oozhi occur, if a person does not change from the principles he holds, faces the suffering and stands firm, he will be praised as an ocean of noble character.”

That is the thought contained in this Kural:

> Even if the cosmic age itself shifts, they do not shift;  
> they are called the ocean of noble character.
