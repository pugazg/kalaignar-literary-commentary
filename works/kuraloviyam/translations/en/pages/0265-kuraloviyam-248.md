Vallaan, from the top of the tree, called to Nallaan: “Hey, run off somewhere and disappear. Otherwise the bear will finish you and then find me too.”

“Wherever I go, the bear will not leave me. I am staying right here,” said Nallaan.

“You wretch! If it finds you, it will find me afterward too! Go somewhere else and let yourself be caught in its mouth there. At least save me!” Vallaan cried.

Meanwhile the bear was seen coming toward the bush. Nallaan suddenly dropped to the ground, held his breath and lay as though lifeless. The bear came near and looked at him. It brought its face close to his to see whether he was alive. Deceived into thinking he was dead, the bear went back the way it had come.

A story like this is often told to children in primary school. Let us attach a Kural to that story, alter it slightly and read it anew.

Once the bear had gone, Vallaan climbed down from the tree. By then Nallaan too had risen and sat up, ready to receive his friend. Vallaan looked at Nallaan in wonder and asked:

“My dear friend! That bear whispered some message in your ear. What was it?”

Nallaan laughed. “The bear recited a Kural to me, explained it, and then went away,” he said.

“My sweet friend! What Kural was that?”

Still smiling, Nallaan said it was this one:

> Even in adversity there is one gain: it is a measuring rod  
> stretched out to measure one's friends.

“Is that so? Even a bear knows the Kural? And what explanation did it give you?” Vallaan asked.

Nallaan gave the explanation as well:

“Even in the harm that comes to us there is a kind of benefit. How? That very harm becomes the measuring rod that measures and reveals to us the true nature of our friends.”

Hearing this explanation, Vallaan lowered his head in shame.
