He was the owner of two boats. A handsome young bull of a man. Broad forehead. Sharp eyes. A budding moustache above slightly full lips. A victorious smile always on his face.

When he walked with shoulders raised, the eyes of women passing him in the street would linger on him before turning away. He was the owner of two boats.

He would take up the spear, go to the battlefield and return after displaying valour. He took pride in glorious wounds from which blood welled. The blood seeping from those wounds would turn the river in which he bathed red. Seeing his strength of mind, lotus-like women surrendered their hearts and stood motionless on the riverbank like statues. He was the owner of two boats.

He would take up a palm leaf and shape poems, letting them emerge as music and fall like a rain of honey. Young women would lose themselves in that sweetness. He was the owner of two boats.

The whole town would gather and sing his fame. In elegant classical Tamil he would cool like a breeze—and rise also like a storm without wings. Fish-eyed women with slender waists would attack him like honeybees. He was the owner of two boats.

Without lifting his eyes toward anyone, yielding to none, no matter how many flowers bloomed before him or how many peacocks danced, he remained unmoved and travelled back and forth in his two boats. He was the owner of two boats.

Then one woman invaded him and shattered his stubbornness. In that siege of love, at last he was defeated. He rushed toward her, trembling to embrace her. She leaned upon his chest and seemed to disappear into him. She brushed his shoulder and flew away. He touched her; she darted upward and seemed to float in the sky. He asked for a kiss; she gave one. He asked for it every day; she hid herself, letting only the sound of her bangles betray her.

The sickness of love seized him. It burned as the fire of desire.

He was the owner of two boats. He ran to cross the flood of love by climbing into one of them.

He ran and ran.

The two boats were nowhere to be seen.
