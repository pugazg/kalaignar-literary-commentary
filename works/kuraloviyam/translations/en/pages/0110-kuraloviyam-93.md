—his eyes, people said, opened only upon books. At night, after staying awake for a long time, his eyes would slowly close only in the company of literary works. His very house looked like a vast library, they said. There were no books in the world that he had not finished reading—unless someone were to write new ones hereafter. So much had he read. Literature, historical records, research collections, epics and puranas, astronomy, geography—he was a great scholar learned in many such fields. Once word spread that such a man was coming to deliver an address, was there any need to ask whether a great crowd would gather?

Without making the people wait long, he too came and took his seat on the stage. The organizer of the association rose and introduced the speaker to those assembled before him, scattering a few words of praise.

> “What we have learned is a handful of earth—  
> what we have not learned is the measure of the world,”

people say. But in contrast to that, of today's speaker one may say, “What he has learned is world-sized; what he has not learned is only a handful of earth.” One so learned has come to honour our assembly. He will now address us.”

Following the organizer's announcement, applause rose like the sound of the sea. The distinguished speaker began his address.

“People formed of love!” he called out, then cleared his throat. Literary quotations served his flood of words. Historical evidence came piling in. The speech went on and on. Before even a few nazhigai had passed from its beginning, a quarter of the people in the hall slowly rose and went out.

He continued speaking. His speech sounded like someone reading books aloud, a little louder than usual.

Before he had finished his address, three-quarters of the gathering had dispersed. The association organizer stood up and appealed to those leaving: “Please be gracious enough to remain seated! Listen to these rare ideas! Those who have gone may go, but at least those who remain should stay and listen; that will be the honour we can offer this distinguished speaker.” In obedience to that appeal, those seated in the first three rows did not rise. But they kept twisting their bodies from time to time. Many yawned. Some propped their heads on their hands and slept. Now and then the sound of snoring arose. Somehow the speech came to an end. Those still left in the hall departed for home with the feeling of having been released.
