—the field was cracked all across, scars of drought. While hundreds of lamps burned brightly all around, why did that one field alone lie dark like a lamp that had never known oil?

The owner of that land was the adopted child of laziness. Rather than labouring and rejoicing in work, he delighted in sleep. Wherever he found a veranda, he shut his eyes. After sowing a crop in name alone and coming away, he never turned to look toward the field again.

Did he at least ease the longing of the lady of his house? Not even that. “Will he not stay near me? Will he not lie here drinking in my beauty?” his life-partner would yearn and melt. He tormented that slender vine just as he neglected his wet field.

Whenever that “waster of time” happened to come home, the moon-faced woman's eyes would redden—not with desire but with disgust. She would not speak a word. She would engage in deep lovers' sulking. Was it not natural that her moon-face should turn into a ball of fire?

That evening too it was the same. He came and stood at the doorway, calling to his wife with affection. She did not come running to welcome him with overflowing desire, saying, “Atthaan has come!” Yet she peeped out. Her eyes flew past the shape of her husband and toward the street. There, from the threshing floors, a procession of carts loaded with sacks of paddy was coming.

Her heart seethed. She cast upon her husband a look in which pain and disgust were intertwined. “Are you even a man? You have no time to look at me; could you not at least have gone and fondled the maiden of the field? Has ruin not come because she has sulked? Must we now starve the whole year with our stomachs clinging empty?”

Without pouring out words, she poured all this through her eyes—as though it were honey.

Was her angry look not justified?

If a wife sulks, perhaps not at one moment but at another her heart will change and she will show a flower-like face. But if the maiden of the land is not visited and cared for again and again in the proper season—if she is not bathed in the water of labour—she will sulk in a way that burns the farmer's very life in poverty.

That is why Valluvar says that the field's sulking is cruel like the sulking of one's wedded wife—indeed crueller still.

> If the landowner does not go to her, the land takes offence  
> and sulks like the wife within his house.

Chapter 104 — Agriculture; Kural 1039
