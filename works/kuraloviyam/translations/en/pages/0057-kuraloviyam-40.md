—why did you choose the sword for the comparison?” That was the student’s question. Valluvar answered him.

“Dear one! Read the Kural deeply, not just once but twice. Then what I am saying will become clear. Through this Kural I emphasize that concealed enmity—hiding itself, behaving like kin and suddenly striking—is far more dangerous than open hostility; and that even if one need not fear an open enemy, one must fear hidden enmity and conduct oneself with caution.”

After hearing Valluvar’s explanation, the student raised another question.

“Great one! Even a sword is visible only at certain times. At all other times, is it not hidden inside its sheath? Seen that way, would it not be more fitting to compare concealed, striking enmity to a sword? How then have you compared open enmity to a sword?”

Valluvar replied with a smile.

“Even when a sword is in its sheath, it does not completely conceal itself. Its large hilt still thrusts its head outside the sheath. But there are enemies who, unlike the sword, hide themselves completely, behave as friends and kinsmen, and wait for the right moment to lift a stone and drop it on one’s head. That is why I said that even if one does not fear an enemy ‘like a sword,’ one must fear the connection of an enemy ‘like kin.’”

Valluvar’s answer plunged the student into a sea of delight.

Yes! A sword that lies in its sheath may be drawn and swung at us. Or a sword already freed from its sheath and held in someone’s hand may leap at us at any moment. Since this can be known in advance, there is no need for fear. But those who live among us as our own people, form bonds of friendship and wait for the moment to cause ruin—one must give such enmity no opening at all, fear it, and remain protected in advance.

This Kural portrays that beautifully:

> Fear not the enemy who stands like a sword; fear instead  
> The bond of the enemy who behaves like kin.

Chapter 89 — Internal Enmity; Kural 882
