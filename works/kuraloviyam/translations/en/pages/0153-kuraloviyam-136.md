## Visual material

Large colour illustration above the prose showing a woman in the foreground with a second female face inset, visually accompanying the woman-addressing-her-heart vignette.

“My sweet heart! Shame on me—look at me calling you a sweet heart! You endure so many troubles, yet in this one matter alone you have not even a drop of firmness. You have accepted him as yours—but why do you have so many doubts about him? Is that not why you so often take up a lovers' quarrel? And does even that quarrel possess any firmness that will last? What do you do the moment you see him? While I am quarrelling with him, accusing him of an offence I have not properly investigated and understood—once his hands touch me, where do you disappear to, my heart? He embraces me tightly—he writes a literature of love—and, unable to push him away, I give myself wholly to him. In the chariot-festival called union, the quarrel becomes like a coin that has somehow vanished from circulation.

“When I see him, the one who ends the quarrel and delights me through union, is it not you, my heart, who should stand in the way, restrain me, make me remain angry with him and keep away? Instead, at that very moment you disappear somewhere! Yet when he is absent, you leap up in false anger against him!”

This Kural speaks of just such a sweet-voiced woman addressing her own heart:

> My heart, when you see the lover who joins and ends our quarrel, you forget to sulk;  
> yet when he is away, you burn with a false anger.

Chapter 125 — Speaking with the Heart; Kural 1246
