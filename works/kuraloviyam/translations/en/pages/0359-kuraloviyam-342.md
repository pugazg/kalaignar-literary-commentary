> A ruler who takes to harassment and conducts himself in injustice  
> is crueller than those who have taken murder as their trade.

Valluvar explains just rule as the act of removing the cruel man like a weed and protecting the crop that is the citizenry. He states just as firmly that a government that destroys the crop itself—the citizens—is worse than the cruel murderer who is the weed.

Does not Valluvar thereby indicate that such a government, more harmful than the weed itself, must be removed?

There was once in Uganda a ruler drunk on power who took pleasure in destroying the very crop of citizens, as weeds destroy a field.

His name was Idi Amin.

Only a few years earlier that country had won full independence. A capable president named Obote had taken charge.

Without recognizing the wolf that lived in Idi Amin's heart, Obote appointed him as his trusted commander. While Obote was abroad, the frenzied Idi Amin seized power. There was no antic he did not perform, no grotesque dance he did not dance. Carrying the burden of government responsibility on his head, he staged a spectacle that the whole world watched in pain. He played ball with the heads of those he disliked. He bathed daily in blood. He took pleasure in women. He even ate their flesh. The death sentences he imposed without inquiries were beyond counting.

“Those who oppose me will not be alive tomorrow!”—this became his practice.

Citizens were hunted every day. Merely criticizing him was enough; a grave would be waiting. What was the end? This sinner who claimed to be removing weeds while destroying the crop itself was crueller than murder-crazed killers. “Why should this man still have the flag of rule?” cried the worms that had been trampled, rising in rebellion as tigers.

The clown Idi Amin fled and disappeared; no one knew where he hid.

This is the final end of a government that carries on tyranny in the name of just rule. Does not the history of the wretch Idi Amin illustrate the difference between the two Kurals cited here?
