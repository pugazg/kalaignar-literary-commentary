“Yes! I thought of you and of some of those who used to surround you. I changed only one letter in the Kural. It suits your life very well,” he replied, and then began explaining the meaning of his altered Kural.

“‘Even when one has fallen low’ = even when a person is in a period of decline—

‘cherishing enmity’ = acting with hostile feeling—

‘is found among one's kindred’ = this is the special quality seen among those who go on calling themselves his relatives, calling him elder brother and younger brother, and staying around him.”

I laughed on hearing my mischievous friend's explanation of the Kural. “Have all who surrounded me become like that? Have people like you disappeared?” I asked, and embraced him as tears gathered in my eyes. His eyes too became waterfalls.

“No decline has come to me. Standing up against suffering is not decline. One needs a kind of wealth even to resist it. I possess plenty of that wealth. It is the wealth called firmness of heart,” I explained to him.

“I do not know. Some of those who surrounded you made themselves appear as though they were truly your kin. The moment harm came to you, not only did they run away, they did not fail to show ‘enmity’ either. That is why I altered the Kural a little,” said my friend.

“Friend, see what a disastrous meaning has arisen because you placed the word ‘enmity’ where the word ‘old ties’ ought to be! Why alter the Kural for the sake of those few? The Kural that already exists rings out for true friends like you. Friends such as you are my kindred. Valluvar wrote the Kural to explain that the nature of kin is to speak with affection of long-standing ties even when a person falls into hardship. So please forget the Kural you corrected—leave your corrected Kural aside and recite the Thirukkural,” I said.

And he recited the Thirukkural.

It is this:

> Even when one has fallen low, cherishing old ties  
> is found among one's kindred.
