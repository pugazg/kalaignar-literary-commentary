Nor would the young man abandon in midstream the woman who had trusted him.

Unable to prevail against his parents, he fled to another town with that shining golden statue of a woman.

His father, raging that family honour had been lost, broke his heart and died.

Hearing the news from afar, the son was shattered.

“How can I, who caused my father's death, ever show my face to my mother?”

he grieved.

Several years rolled by.

He and the woman became father and mother themselves. They had a child like a little parrot.

One night—at midnight—they gathered their courage and returned to the village with his wife and their flower-like child.

Hearing a knock at the door, the mother rose, removed the bar and opened it.

With the help of the small oil lamp in her hand, she looked at their faces.

“Mother!”

The events of the past flashed through her mind like streaks of lightning and vanished.

The door had a bar holding it shut. She had to remove the bar before she could open the door.

But is there any bar that can hold back the tear that rises in the eyes when love reaches its peak?

No. None at all.

When love bursts forth, that bar opens by itself, and the flood of love appears in the form of tears.

“Mother!”

The moment she heard that sound, the son's fault disappeared and love revealed itself.

This Kural paints that moving scene:

> Is there a bar that can shut in love?  
> The tear-filled eyes of the loving will reveal it.

Chapter 8 — Possession of Love; Kural 71

பூசல் தரும் = will reveal
