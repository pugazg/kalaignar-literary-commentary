He and cleanliness had nothing in common. Yet when it came to talking about the faults of others, he had an incurable appetite for it. It was his recreation.

Carrying a mountain of dirt on his own clothes and body, he habitually took a mustard-seed-sized dark spot on another person and enlarged it to the size of the sea.

Once, after roaming around the village, he returned home and went straight to the bedroom. To take something from a shelf fixed against the wall, he climbed onto the bed.

Hearing a sound, his mother looked out from the kitchen. The moment she saw him standing on the bed, she was alarmed. Leaving her work where it was, she came running. By then he had climbed down.

But the bed, which had been as clean as a spread of jasmine flowers, now lay marked here and there by the blackened prints of his dirty feet.

“Why, boy, can you not have even a sesame-tip's worth of responsibility? Is this how you ruin a beautiful bed? How many times have I told you to wash your feet as soon as you come home from outside? I tell you every day. Why do you refuse to reform?”

The mother lamented and scolded him.

“What does it matter how much you advise him or beat him? He keeps going his own way! But Valluvar did not fail to speak even about a person with this nature. Do you know what it is like when someone comes after stepping in mud outside and, without washing his feet, steps upon a bed and stains it? It is like an ignorant man entering an assembly of learned people as though he knew everything! The Kural says there is no greater folly. Let us see whether at least now our son frees himself from that folly.”

Her husband entered as he said this.

This Kural is the picture that explains the scene:

> A fool entering an assembly of the learned  
> is like placing an unwashed foot upon a bed.

Chapter 84 — Folly; Kural 840
